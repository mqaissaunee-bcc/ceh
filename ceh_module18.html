CEH v13 Module 18: IoT and OT Hacking

IoT and OT Hacking

Where cyberattacks reach into the physical world: the insecure IoT device landscape and the Mirai botnet, industrial control systems (SCADA, PLCs, HMIs) and their unauthenticated protocols, landmark ICS attacks from Stuxnet to FrostyGoop, and why segmentation is the defense that matters most.

🏭 Overview

💡Think of it like this

IT failures cost money; OT failures cost limbs.

This is the module where the CIA triad inverts. In IT, confidentiality usually leads. In a plant, availability and integrity dominate, because a turbine that stops or a sensor reading altered by two degrees has physical consequences. Safety, not secrecy, sits at the top of the priority list.

Where the analogy breaks down: The inversion is real but the separation isn't. Convergence means the plant network touches the corporate network, and most OT incidents begin as thoroughly ordinary IT compromises.

When hacking reaches the physical world

Every module so far has attacked information — data to steal, accounts to hijack, systems to control. This module attacks the world where bits meet atoms. It covers two related domains that share a defining trait: they connect computers to physical reality. IoT (the Internet of Things) is the vast population of internet-connected everyday devices — cameras, thermostats, doorbells, sensors, medical devices — now numbering in the tens of billions. OT (Operational Technology) is the hardware and software that runs industrial processes — the power grids, water treatment plants, factories, and pipelines that civilization physically depends on. What makes both so consequential is that a successful attack doesn't just leak data; it can turn off a camera, unlock a door, stop a production line, or damage a turbine. The stakes cross from the digital into the physical, and in OT, into human safety.

Why these are the hardest things to secure

IoT and OT share a set of properties that make them uniquely difficult, and understanding them frames the whole module. They are built for function, not security — a cheap camera or a decades-old industrial controller was engineered to do its job reliably and inexpensively, with security an afterthought or absent entirely. They are rarely patched — many can't be updated at all, and OT hardware routinely runs for 15 to 30 years. They are deployed at enormous scale and often invisibly — organizations frequently don't even know every device on their network. And they can't simply be turned off or rebooted to fix — you can't reboot a running water plant. These constraints mean the security playbook from the rest of the course often doesn't apply, and defenders must lean on isolation and compensating controls instead.

Two domains, different stakes

Though related, IoT and OT differ in what a compromise threatens, and it's worth fixing the distinction early. IoT compromise typically threatens data and connectivity: a hijacked camera spies, a botnet of infected devices launches DDoS attacks, a smart lock is bypassed. OT compromise threatens physical operations and safety: a manipulated controller can halt production, destroy equipment, cut power to a city, or endanger lives. The two overlap in Industrial IoT (IIoT) — connected sensors and devices in industrial settings — but the essential difference in stakes explains why OT security inverts the usual priorities, putting availability and safety ahead of confidentiality. This module treats each domain in turn, then unifies them at the defense layer.

What this module covers

We start with the IoT landscape — what these devices are, why they're so insecure, and the OWASP IoT Top 10. Then IoT attacks — from default-credential compromise to the Mirai botnet and firmware exploitation. We shift to OT and ICS fundamentals — SCADA, PLCs, HMIs, the insecure industrial protocols, and the IT/OT convergence problem. Then OT attacks and landmark incidents — the real-world ICS attacks from Stuxnet onward that define the field, and the 2026 threat reality. We survey the specialized tools, and close with countermeasures, where a single idea — segmentation and isolation — does most of the work for both domains. Throughout, the emphasis is on a world where a vulnerability can have physical, even life-threatening, consequences.

OT testing can hurt people — extreme caution required. This is the one area of the course where a careless action can cause physical harm. Actively scanning or probing an operational technology system — even gently — can crash fragile industrial devices, halt production, or trigger safety incidents; ordinary IT tools like a basic port scan have knocked ICS equipment offline. OT assessment is done with specialized care, often passively, by trained specialists, in coordination with operators and never on live safety-critical systems without explicit authorization and safeguards. For IoT, test only devices you own. Treat this domain with a respect the others don't demand.

🌐 The IoT Landscape

💡Think of it like this

IoT is what happens when a device with a twelve-year service life ships with three months of software support.

A smart camera is a Linux computer with a network stack, default credentials, and a manufacturer who may not exist in two years. Nobody patches a doorbell. The result is millions of internet-connected computers running frozen firmware full of publicly documented vulnerabilities.

Where the analogy breaks down: This is improving under regulation. The UK PSTI Act and the EU Cyber Resilience Act ban universal default passwords and mandate support periods, so new devices are meaningfully better than the installed base suggests.

Computers in everything

The Internet of Things is what happens when you put a small computer and a network connection into ordinary objects. Security cameras, video doorbells, smart TVs, thermostats, baby monitors, smart locks, fitness trackers, connected medical devices, industrial sensors, badge readers — anything with an IP address that isn't a traditional computer or phone. There are now tens of billions of these devices, and the number keeps climbing. Each one is, underneath, a networked computer running software — which means each one is a potential target and a potential foothold onto the network it sits on. The scale alone reframes the security problem: even a tiny per-device failure rate, multiplied across billions of devices, yields an enormous attack surface.

Why IoT is so insecure

IoT devices are, as a category, notoriously insecure, and the reasons are structural rather than accidental:

🔑 Default & weak credentials

The defining IoT flaw. Devices ship with default passwords (admin/admin, root/root) that users never change, or with hardcoded credentials that can't be changed at all. Trivial access at scale.

🔧 No updates

Many devices have no update mechanism, or manufacturers abandon them quickly. Known vulnerabilities stay unpatched for the device's entire life — often many years.

💰 Built to a price

Cheap devices are engineered for cost and function, with security cut to save money. Weak or absent encryption, insecure interfaces, and exposed debug services are common.

👁️ Invisible & unmanaged

"Set-and-forget" devices — cameras, HVAC sensors, badge readers — get installed and forgotten, often unknown to the security team, monitored by no one, and never assessed.

Combine these and you get devices that are easy to compromise, impossible to patch, and invisible to defenders — a nearly ideal target.

The OWASP IoT Top 10

As with web and mobile, OWASP maintains an IoT Top 10 that ranks the most critical IoT security risks, giving testers and manufacturers a shared framework. Its number one entry says everything about the state of the field: Weak, Guessable, or Hardcoded Passwords. The rest of the list catalogs the structural problems above — insecure network services, insecure ecosystem interfaces (the web, cloud, and mobile APIs that manage devices), lack of secure update mechanisms, use of insecure or outdated components, insufficient privacy protection, insecure data transfer and storage, poor device management, insecure default settings, and lack of physical hardening. Notice the pattern: nearly every entry is a basic security practice that ordinary computers got right decades ago, simply absent from IoT. The framework is less a list of exotic attacks than a catalog of fundamentals ignored.

The device as a foothold

One strategic point shapes how attackers think about IoT: the device is often valuable not for itself but as a way in. A compromised smart camera may hold little of direct value, but it sits on the same network as systems that do — and because it's trusted, unmonitored, and rarely inspected, it makes an excellent quiet foothold from which to pivot deeper. This is especially dangerous in organizations where IoT devices share networks with sensitive systems, and it's the bridge to the OT world: a compromised IoT sensor in an industrial facility can be the first step toward the control systems. With the landscape established, the next section examines how these devices are actually attacked.

💥 IoT Attacks

💡Think of it like this

Mirai didn't exploit anything. It logged in.

The most consequential IoT botnet in history scanned for telnet and tried a list of roughly sixty default credentials. That was the attack in its entirety. Beyond credentials there is firmware pulled from a chip and read for hardcoded keys, unauthenticated APIs, and radio protocols such as Zigbee and BLE with weak pairing.

Where the analogy breaks down: Physical attacks assume physical access, which is a genuine constraint for a device inside somebody's home — but not for a parking meter, an EV charger, or a sensor in a field.

The attacker's workflow

Attacking IoT devices follows a recognizable progression that mirrors the broader methodology, adapted to hardware. It begins with discovery — finding the devices, often using Shodan (the search engine for internet-connected devices, introduced in the footprinting module), which indexes exposed cameras, routers, and industrial systems worldwide. Then access — most often through default or weak credentials, but also via known CVEs in the device firmware, exposed services, or insecure management interfaces. Then exploitation — using the device to spy, to steal data, to pivot into the network, or to enlist it into a botnet. The remarkable thing about IoT attacks is how simple the first steps usually are: a large fraction of real compromises begin with nothing more sophisticated than trying admin/admin.

Mirai and the botnet threat

The defining IoT attack is the botnet, and its archetype is Mirai. In 2016, Mirai infected hundreds of thousands of IoT devices — mostly cameras and home routers — using a breathtakingly simple method: it scanned the internet and tried a short list of default username/password combinations. Devices that accepted them were enrolled into a botnet that launched some of the largest DDoS attacks ever recorded, taking down major internet services. Mirai's lasting significance is twofold. It proved that insecure IoT enables internet-scale attacks — the weakness of countless small devices aggregating into overwhelming force. And its source code was released, spawning an ongoing family of variants that continue to this day: as recently as 2026, Mirai-based botnets have been actively recruiting vulnerable IP cameras across finance, healthcare, and transportation. The blueprint Mirai established — scan, guess default passwords, enlist, attack — remains devastatingly effective.

Firmware attacks

A more advanced vector targets the device's firmware — the low-level software that runs it. Because IoT firmware is often poorly protected, an attacker can frequently extract and analyze it (pulling it from the device or downloading a manufacturer update) to reverse-engineer how the device works, and in doing so discover hardcoded credentials, cryptographic keys, backdoors, and vulnerabilities baked into the code. Beyond analysis, attackers may modify firmware — injecting malicious code and re-flashing the device — to gain persistent, low-level control that survives reboots and is very hard to detect. Firmware analysis is a core IoT testing skill precisely because so much of a device's security (and so many of its secrets) live at that level, invisible from the outside but exposed to anyone who extracts the image.

The physical and radio dimension

Unlike purely networked targets, IoT devices are physical objects an attacker can often hold in their hands, which opens attack surfaces the rest of the course rarely considers. Hardware interfaces meant for debugging — UART, JTAG, and serial ports on the circuit board — frequently give direct, privileged access to the device if an attacker can physically reach them, bypassing all network security. And because many IoT devices communicate over radio protocols beyond Wi-Fi — Bluetooth Low Energy, Zigbee, Z-Wave, LoRa — they can be attacked over the air by someone within range, intercepting or injecting the wireless commands that control them. This physical and radio dimension makes IoT testing a distinctly hands-on discipline, blending hardware, radio, and software skills.

Where IoT attacks lead

Step back and IoT attacks serve three broad ends: surveillance and data theft (spying through cameras and microphones, stealing the data devices handle), disruption (botnets and DDoS, or simply disabling devices), and pivoting (using the device as a foothold to reach more valuable systems). That third purpose is the bridge to this module's second half. In an industrial setting, a compromised IoT sensor or camera isn't just a spy — it's a potential doorway into the operational technology that runs the physical process. To understand what waits on the other side of that doorway, we need to understand OT and industrial control systems.

🏭 OT & ICS Fundamentals

💡Think of it like this

A PLC was designed to be reliable in an era when “networked” meant a cable to the next room.

Protocols such as Modbus and DNP3 have no authentication because there was nothing to authenticate against — the wire itself was the boundary. Purdue model levels describe the separation that is supposed to exist between plant floor and business network. A PLC has no antivirus and often cannot be patched without downtime costing more than the risk.

Where the analogy breaks down: The air gap that justified this design mostly doesn't exist. Remote support, wireless sensors and USB drives cross it routinely, so the assumption of isolation has itself become the vulnerability.

The systems that run the physical world

Operational Technology is the hardware and software that monitors and controls physical industrial processes — the machinery of power generation, water treatment, manufacturing, oil and gas, and transportation. The systems that do this control are collectively called Industrial Control Systems (ICS), and they are the beating heart of critical infrastructure. When you flip a light switch, drink tap water, or drive across a bridge with working traffic signals, OT made it happen. This is why OT security is a matter of national importance: an attack here doesn't inconvenience users, it can darken a city or contaminate a water supply. Understanding the components is the prerequisite to understanding both the attacks and why they're so serious.

The building blocks

🎛️ SCADA

Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition — the overarching systems that monitor and control industrial processes, often across large geographic areas (a whole pipeline or power grid). The supervisory brain of the operation.

⚙️ PLCs

Programmable Logic Controllers — the rugged, specialized computers that directly control physical machinery: opening valves, starting motors, running production steps. The hands that touch the process. Stuxnet's target.

🖥️ HMIs

Human-Machine Interfaces — the screens operators use to view the state of the process and issue commands. Compromising an HMI lets an attacker both manipulate the process and deceive the operators about what's happening.

📟 RTUs & sensors

Remote Terminal Units and field sensors/actuators — the distributed devices that gather readings and carry out commands out in the field, often connected back over cellular or radio links.

The insecure protocols

Here is the technical crux of OT insecurity. Industrial systems communicate using specialized protocols — Modbus, DNP3, PROFINET, BACnet, IEC 61850, EtherNet/IP — and these protocols were designed decades ago, for a world of isolated, physically-secured, trusted networks. They were built for reliability and real-time performance, and they generally have no authentication and no encryption. The consequence is stark: any attacker who can reach these protocols on the network can typically read the data and issue commands to machinery directly, with the device obediently doing whatever it's told, because it has no way to verify who is talking to it. There is no password to crack because there is no password. This design was reasonable when these networks were air-gapped islands — and it is catastrophic now that they're increasingly connected.

The IT/OT convergence problem

Which brings us to the central problem of modern OT security: convergence. For decades, OT networks were air-gapped — physically isolated from corporate IT and the internet — and that isolation, not any built-in security, was what kept them safe. The drive for efficiency, remote monitoring, and data analytics has steadily connected these once-isolated networks to IT and to the internet, removing the isolation without adding the security. Now an attacker who breaches the softer corporate IT network can potentially pivot into the OT network, where the unauthenticated protocols and unpatchable 30-year-old controllers offer little resistance. Missing or poor IT/OT segmentation is repeatedly identified as the single most common root cause of OT compromise following an IT intrusion. The 2026 reality makes this vivid: the US hosts the highest count of internet-accessible ICS ports globally, including unauthenticated Modbus and DNP3 systems reachable from anywhere.

Why OT can't be secured like IT

Finally, the principle that governs all OT defense: OT inverts the usual security priorities. In IT, confidentiality often leads. In OT, availability and safety come first — an industrial process must not stop (downtime can cost millions or endanger people) and must never cause physical harm. This inversion has hard consequences for security. You often can't patch a running production system, because taking it down or risking instability is unacceptable. You can't reboot a water plant to apply an update. You may not be able to install security software on a 20-year-old controller at all. Traditional IT security, which assumes you can patch, reboot, and update freely, simply doesn't fit. This is why OT defense leans so heavily on isolation and compensating controls rather than direct hardening — a theme the countermeasures section develops.

⚠️ OT Attacks & Landmark Incidents

💡Think of it like this

Stuxnet lied to the operators while it broke the machines.

It altered centrifuge speeds while replaying normal readings to the monitoring screens, so the process was destroyed and the displays looked entirely healthy. That is the template: manipulate the physical process, then manipulate the view of it. Ukraine in 2015 opened breakers remotely, and TRITON attacked the safety system itself.

Where the analogy breaks down: These were state-resourced operations against specific targets. Everyday OT incidents are far more mundane — ransomware hits the IT side and operations halt because production cannot be scheduled or billed.

Learning OT security through its history

OT security is best understood through the real attacks that have shaped it, because unlike most of the course, this field has a small number of landmark incidents that each taught the world something new — and each caused, or threatened, physical consequences. These aren't hypotheticals; they're the case studies every OT security professional knows. Walking through them chronologically shows both how these attacks work and how the threat has escalated from isolated sabotage to systematic nation-state pre-positioning.

Stuxnet (2010): the one that changed everything

The landmark that inaugurated the field. Stuxnet was a highly sophisticated worm — widely attributed to nation-state actors — designed to sabotage Iran's nuclear program. It spread via USB drives and networks, but remained dormant until it detected a very specific target: particular Siemens PLC configurations controlling uranium-enrichment centrifuges. When it found them, it altered the PLCs' logic to physically destroy the centrifuges by subtly varying their speed, while feeding operators falsified readings so everything looked normal. Stuxnet's significance is hard to overstate: it was the first widely-known cyberattack to cause physical destruction in the real world, proving that code could break machines. It demonstrated the man-in-the-middle deception of operators, the targeting of specific industrial hardware, and the strategic use of a cyberweapon — and it opened the era of ICS-targeted attacks.

The lineage that followed

Stuxnet was the first of a growing family of ICS-focused attacks, each extending the threat:

⚡ BlackEnergy (2015)

Used against Ukraine's power grid, entering via phishing and targeting the SCADA systems managing substations — cutting power to hundreds of thousands of people. The first confirmed cyberattack to cause a blackout.

🔌 Industroyer / Industroyer2 (2016, 2022)

Malware purpose-built to speak industrial protocols directly, automating attacks on electric grid equipment. Industroyer2 was deployed against Ukraine again in 2022.

🧨 Pipedream / Incontroller (2022)

A modular ICS attack toolkit targeting Schneider and OMRON PLCs — notable as a flexible, reusable framework rather than a one-off, signaling the industrialization of OT attack capability.

❄️ FrostyGoop (2024)

Used Modbus TCP — the unauthenticated industrial protocol — to attack heating systems, cutting heat to civilians in winter. A stark demonstration of the protocol weakness in action.

The attack path

Across these incidents a common path emerges, and it's the one defenders must break. Attackers rarely start in the OT network directly; instead they follow the convergence gap. Initial access comes through the softer IT side — phishing, credential stuffing, or a compromised vendor (supply chain is now a leading initial-access vector for OT). From there they achieve lateral movement into OT through shared VPNs, jump boxes, or mis-segmented VLANs — exploiting exactly the missing segmentation identified earlier. Once in the OT network, the unauthenticated protocols mean manipulating the process is often straightforward: issue commands to PLCs, alter their logic, or falsify what operators see on HMIs. Understanding this chain — IT foothold, pivot to OT, manipulate the physical process — is what makes the countermeasures make sense.

The 2026 reality

The threat has escalated sharply and is very much current. Multiple 2026 government advisories — including a joint FBI, CISA, NSA, and allied-agency warning — describe foreign-affiliated actors actively targeting internet-exposed PLCs across critical infrastructure, with the intent to cause disruption by manipulating controller logic and altering what operators see. Security researchers have found thousands of internet-exposed industrial controllers. Most concerning, nation-state actors are engaged in pre-positioning — quietly establishing footholds inside energy, water, and telecom OT environments to be activated later — and ransomware groups have industrialized OT targeting, now fielding operators who understand PLC logic and SCADA architecture. Meanwhile, AI is compressing the attack cycle, helping adversaries automate reconnaissance and protocol analysis. This is not a historical topic; it is one of the most active and consequential fronts in security today.

🛠️ Tools

💡Think of it like this

Scan an OT network the way you scan an IT network and you may stop the plant.

A PLC with a fragile stack can crash on a standard nmap scan. So the tooling differs: Shodan and Censys for external exposure, passive protocol analysis using Wireshark's Modbus dissectors, and firmware analysis with binwalk on a bench. Testing happens on the test rig or during planned downtime.

Where the analogy breaks down: Even passive work carries risk. Adding a tap or a span port means touching the network, and a change window on a production line is measured against lost output — so OT assessment is as much a scheduling and safety exercise as a technical one.

A different toolkit for a physical world

IoT and OT testing draws on some familiar tools and adds specialized ones for hardware, radio, and industrial protocols. A crucial caveat frames everything here: in OT especially, tools must be used with extreme care, because active scanning can crash fragile devices and disrupt physical processes. Much OT assessment is therefore passive — observing traffic rather than probing — and the tooling reflects that. We'll group the tools by what they examine: discovery, firmware, radio, and industrial protocols.

Discovery and analysis

🔎 Shodan

The search engine for internet-connected devices. It indexes exposed cameras, routers, and — alarmingly — industrial control systems worldwide, letting a tester (or attacker) find exposed IoT and ICS by type, location, and vulnerability. Essential reconnaissance, and a sobering way to see how much is exposed.

🧬 Firmware analysis

Binwalk extracts and analyzes firmware images, revealing embedded file systems, hardcoded secrets, and code; Firmwalker and firmware-analysis frameworks automate the hunt for credentials and keys. The core of understanding a device from its firmware.

📡 SDR & radio tools

Software-Defined Radio (like HackRF) plus tools for Bluetooth, Zigbee, and Z-Wave let testers capture and analyze the wireless protocols many IoT devices use — attacking the over-the-air layer that network tools can't see.

🔌 Hardware interfaces

Bus Pirate, JTAG/UART adapters, and logic analyzers connect to the debug and serial ports on a device's circuit board — the physical access that often bypasses all network security to reach a root shell.

Industrial protocol tools

For OT specifically, specialized tools understand the industrial protocols. Wireshark (from the sniffing module) parses Modbus, DNP3, and other ICS protocols, making passive traffic analysis — the safest way to assess an OT network — genuinely informative. Frameworks and scripts exist to interact with Modbus and other protocols directly (some bundled into Metasploit and dedicated ICS toolkits), but these are handled with great caution in live environments because issuing commands to real controllers can have physical consequences. The safest and most common OT assessment technique is passive protocol analysis: quietly listening to the industrial traffic to identify devices, protocols, and weaknesses without ever transmitting to fragile equipment. This restraint is itself a professional skill.

The AI dimension and the caution that defines this domain

As across CEH v13, AI increasingly assists here — helping analyze extracted firmware, reverse-engineer unfamiliar device code, and parse captured industrial traffic. But the defining feature of this domain's tooling isn't any tool; it's restraint. In every other module, running a scanner aggressively is at worst noisy. Here, it can crash a device that controls a physical process, and in OT that can mean halted production or a safety incident. The professional discipline of IoT/OT testing is knowing when not to act — favoring passive observation, coordinating with operators, working on test benches rather than live systems, and treating the possibility of physical harm as the constant governing constraint. The tools are powerful; the judgment about when to use them is what matters most.

Active scanning can cause physical harm. Unlike anywhere else in the course, a routine scan here can knock an industrial device offline and disrupt a physical process — basic IT tools have crashed ICS equipment. Never actively test OT systems without explicit authorization, operator coordination, and safeguards; prefer passive analysis; and practice on your own IoT devices, test benches, and dedicated ICS lab environments, never on live infrastructure.

🛡️ Countermeasures

💡Think of it like this

If you can't patch it, wrap it.

Compensating controls do the work here: strict segmentation between IT and OT, unidirectional gateways where data must flow out but nothing may flow in, jump hosts for remote access, passive monitoring that never injects traffic, and safety instrumented systems kept on a separate path entirely.

Where the analogy breaks down: Segmentation is a design decision, not a setting. Retrofitting it into a plant assembled over thirty years is a capital project, which is why “just segment it” is honest advice and very rarely quick advice.

Defending the undefendable

IoT and OT present a hard defensive problem: many of the devices can't be secured by conventional means — you can't patch them, install security software on them, or often even change their configuration. This forces a different philosophy. Rather than hardening each device (frequently impossible), the strategy centers on controlling what can reach the devices and knowing what you have. The good news is that a small number of foundational measures — led by one idea, segmentation — defend both domains remarkably well, precisely because they don't depend on the devices being securable.

The foundational defenses

🧱 Segmentation & isolation

The single most important defense for both IoT and OT. Isolate these devices on separate network segments (VLANs), behind firewalls, so they aren't directly reachable from the internet or from general corporate networks. For OT, an industrial DMZ between IT and OT is the standard. Since missing segmentation is the #1 root cause of OT compromise, this is where defense begins.

📋 Asset inventory

You can't protect what you don't know exists. Discovering and cataloging every IoT/OT device — the many undocumented "set-and-forget" devices included — is a genuine first step, and inventories routinely surface devices no one was watching.

🔑 Eliminate default credentials

Change every default password, remove hardcoded ones where possible, and disable unused services and interfaces. This alone defeats the Mirai-style attacks that drive so much IoT compromise.

🔄 Update where feasible

Patch firmware when a mechanism exists; where it doesn't (common in OT), wrap the un-patchable device in compensating controls — external firewalls, proxies, and strict access limits — to protect it in place.

Securing the IT/OT boundary

Because the dominant OT attack path runs from IT into OT, defending that boundary is decisive. Strong IT/OT segmentation with an industrial DMZ ensures that a breach of the corporate network can't flow freely into the control network. Secured remote access — jump servers acting as controlled gateways, with MFA, session logging, and command restrictions — replaces the fragile VPNs and undocumented bridges that attackers exploit. And monitoring purpose-built for OT — passive network monitoring that understands industrial protocols and watches for anomalous commands, without disrupting the process — provides the detection that traditional IT security tools can't. Together these close the convergence gap that every landmark OT attack has exploited.

The defense-in-depth mindset for physical systems

Two further principles reflect the physical stakes. First, safety systems must remain independent: the safety instrumented systems that prevent physical disasters should be separate from the control systems and not remotely accessible, so that even a full control-system compromise can't defeat the last line of defense against a catastrophe. Second, defense assumes the device itself is untrustworthy — since it can't protect itself, the network around it must, through segmentation, monitoring, and access control. This is a humbling inversion of normal security, where you usually can harden the endpoint. Here, you often can't, so you build the security into the environment instead. It's the same logic that governs any system you can't fix: contain what you cannot cure.

The through-line

IoT and OT security comes down to a single reframing: when the devices themselves can't be secured, secure what can reach them. Change the default passwords, know every device you have, and above all segment and isolate — keeping insecure IoT off sensitive networks and keeping OT walled off from IT behind a controlled boundary. Every landmark attack in this module, from Stuxnet to the 2026 PLC-targeting campaigns, ultimately exploited a device that was reachable when it shouldn't have been. The physical stakes make this the highest-consequence domain in the course — where a vulnerability can stop a factory or darken a city — and also, in a sense, the one with the clearest defense: control the pathways, because you can't always fix the endpoints. The final module turns to the mathematical foundation underlying nearly every defense we've discussed: cryptography.
