📋 Module Overview & Learning Objectives

What sniffing is, and why it still matters

Sniffing is the practice of capturing network traffic as it travels across a medium — wired Ethernet, Wi-Fi, or a link between switches — and reading its contents. Every packet a computer sends is, at the physical level, electrical signals or radio waves that anything on the same medium can potentially observe. A network interface normally ignores traffic not addressed to it, but a sniffer puts the interface into promiscuous mode (or monitor mode on wireless), telling it to capture everything it can see rather than only its own traffic. That single change turns an ordinary network card into a surveillance device.

The reason sniffing remains a core topic decades after it was first understood is that the foundational protocols of the internet were designed in an era of implicit trust. ARP, DNS, DHCP, HTTP, Telnet, FTP — none of them were built with authentication or encryption. They assume the network is friendly. When those assumptions hold, sniffing reveals usernames, passwords, session tokens, emails, and confidential data in plaintext. Modern encryption (TLS, VPNs) has closed much of that exposure, but not all of it: metadata is still visible, misconfigurations still leak plaintext, and many internal and legacy systems still speak the old unencrypted protocols. For an attacker, a sniffing foothold on a network is a window into everything crossing it; for a defender, understanding sniffing is the prerequisite to knowing what an attacker can and cannot see.

The central distinction: passive vs. active

Every technique in this module falls into one of two categories, and the distinction governs both how the attack works and how detectable it is.

🔇 Passive sniffing

Simply listening to traffic that already reaches the attacker's interface, without injecting anything onto the network. On a legacy hub — which broadcasts every frame to every port — passive sniffing captures the entire segment's traffic and is essentially undetectable, because the attacker sends nothing. The catch: hubs are obsolete. On a modern switched network, an interface only receives its own traffic and broadcasts, so passive sniffing alone sees very little.

⚡ Active sniffing

Manipulating the network to redirect traffic that would not normally reach the attacker — the techniques needed to defeat a switch. ARP poisoning, MAC flooding, DHCP spoofing, and DNS poisoning all fall here. Because active sniffing injects crafted packets onto the network, it leaves traces and can be detected and prevented with the right switch-level controls. This is the crucial defensive insight: the attacks that actually work on modern networks are also the ones you can catch.

Much of this module is really the story of that arms race: the switch was introduced partly to make casual sniffing harder, attackers developed active techniques to defeat it, and network engineers responded with a stack of switch security features that neutralize those techniques. Understanding each move in that sequence is understanding the module.

Learning objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to:

  • Explain how a switch's CAM table works and how MAC flooding defeats it.
  • Describe ARP poisoning step by step, and why the ARP protocol is vulnerable by design.
  • Distinguish DNS poisoning, DHCP spoofing, and MAC spoofing, and what each achieves for an attacker.
  • Use Wireshark and command-line tools to capture and analyze traffic, including reconstructing credentials from a capture.
  • Configure the switch-level defenses — port security, DHCP snooping, Dynamic ARP Inspection — that stop these attacks, and explain the order they must be enabled in.
  • Recognize why encryption (TLS, VPNs) is the last line of defense that makes captured traffic useless even when sniffing succeeds.

Authorized testing only. Capturing traffic on a network you do not own or administer is illegal wiretapping in most jurisdictions, even if you never act on what you see. The techniques here are for securing networks you are responsible for and for authorized penetration testing under a written agreement. Sniffing tools are dual-use — the same Wireshark capture that a network engineer uses to troubleshoot a slow application can expose a colleague's password — so the ethical line is defined entirely by authorization.

🔍 Sniffing Fundamentals

How data actually moves: the two addresses that matter

To understand sniffing you have to understand how a frame finds its destination, because every sniffing attack is a manipulation of that process. Traffic on a local network is addressed at two layers simultaneously. At Layer 2 (Data Link), devices are identified by MAC addresses — 48-bit hardware addresses burned into each network interface, used to deliver frames within a single local segment. At Layer 3 (Network), devices are identified by IP addresses, used to route packets across networks. A frame traveling from your laptop to a website carries both: the IP address identifies the ultimate destination, while the MAC address identifies the next hop on the local wire.

The protocol that bridges these two layers is ARP (Address Resolution Protocol), and it is the soft underbelly of local networks. When a device knows the IP address it wants to reach but not the corresponding MAC address, it broadcasts an ARP request — "who has 192.168.1.1?" — and the owner replies with its MAC. The device caches that mapping and uses it. The fatal flaw: ARP has no authentication whatsoever. A device will accept and cache any ARP reply it receives, even one it never asked for, and even one that contradicts what it already knows. This single design decision, made when local networks were small and trusted, is what makes ARP poisoning possible — and ARP poisoning is the workhorse of active sniffing.

Hubs vs. switches: why the network topology decides the attack

The device connecting machines on a local segment determines what an attacker can passively see, and the shift from hubs to switches is the reason active sniffing exists.

Hubs — the sniffer's paradise (now extinct)

A hub is electrically dumb: any frame arriving on one port is repeated out every other port. Every device sees every frame and simply ignores those not addressed to it. An attacker in promiscuous mode on a hub captures the entire segment passively and invisibly. Hubs have been obsolete for two decades, but they define the baseline the switch was designed to fix.

Switches — selective delivery

A switch is intelligent: it learns which MAC address lives on which port and forwards each frame only to the port where its destination resides. Traffic between two other machines never reaches the attacker's port at all. This is why passive sniffing largely fails on modern networks — and why an attacker must actively trick the switch into delivering traffic it otherwise would not.

The CAM table: the switch's memory, and its weakness

A switch's intelligence lives in its CAM table (Content Addressable Memory), also called the MAC address table. This is where the switch records the MAC-address-to-port mappings it has learned. When a frame arrives, the switch looks up the destination MAC in the CAM table and forwards the frame out only the matching port. The mechanism is efficient and normally invisible — but it has a hard limit that attackers exploit.

The CAM table is finite; it can hold only a fixed number of entries. When it fills up, the switch faces a choice, and for the sake of not dropping traffic it fails open: an entry it cannot store means it does not know which port the destination is on, so it falls back to flooding the frame out every port — behaving, in effect, like a hub. This fail-open behavior is the entire basis of the MAC flooding attack covered in the Active Sniffing section: an attacker deliberately floods the switch with thousands of fake MAC addresses until the CAM table overflows, forcing the switch to broadcast traffic the attacker can then capture. The switch does exactly what it was designed to do — it just was not designed to expect an adversary filling its memory on purpose.

Why fail-open? A switch could fail closed — refusing to forward frames it can't table — but that would drop legitimate traffic during normal bursts of new devices, breaking the network. Failing open keeps traffic flowing at the cost of security. Port security, covered in Countermeasures, is the feature that changes this trade-off by capping how many MACs a port will learn.

Getting the interface to listen: promiscuous mode

By default, a network interface performs its own filtering: it reads the destination MAC of each incoming frame and discards any that are not addressed to it (or to a broadcast/multicast group it belongs to). Promiscuous mode disables this filtering, instructing the card to hand every frame it receives up to the operating system regardless of addressing. This is what a sniffer requires to capture traffic — and on wired networks it is a purely passive change the attacker makes to their own interface, which is why passive sniffing is so hard to detect. On wireless, the equivalent is monitor mode, which additionally captures frames from all nearby networks, not just the one the card is associated with.

Capturing the frames is only half the job; the other half is analysis — turning millions of raw packets into meaning. That is the role of tools like Wireshark and tcpdump, covered in the Tools section, which parse each protocol layer, reassemble streams, and let an analyst filter for exactly the traffic of interest.

⚡ Active Sniffing Techniques

Defeating the switch

On a switched network, an attacker's interface only receives its own traffic — so to sniff anything interesting, the attacker must actively manipulate the network into delivering traffic it otherwise would not. There are two fundamentally different strategies for this, and understanding which applies when is a key skill. The first is to overwhelm the switch so it reverts to hub-like flooding (MAC flooding). The second, far more surgical, is to lie to the victims about where traffic should go, inserting the attacker into the path (ARP poisoning). ARP poisoning is the workhorse because it is targeted, quiet by comparison, and gives the attacker a true man-in-the-middle position.

MAC flooding: overwhelming the CAM table

Recall from Fundamentals that a switch's CAM table is finite and fails open when full. MAC flooding weaponizes exactly this. The attacker sends a torrent of frames, each with a different fabricated source MAC address. The switch dutifully records each new MAC in its CAM table, and within seconds the table is exhausted. From that moment, any frame whose destination the switch can no longer look up is flooded out every port — including the attacker's. The switch has effectively degraded into a hub, and passive sniffing works again across the whole segment.

MAC flooding is blunt and noisy: it degrades performance for everyone, generates a flood of traffic that monitoring will notice, and affects the entire segment rather than a chosen victim. Tools like macof (part of the dsniff suite) perform it in a single command. Its saving grace for defenders is that the very feature designed to stop it — port security, which limits how many MAC addresses a switch port will learn — is simple to configure and completely neutralizes the attack.

# macof floods the switch with random source MACs to overflow the CAM table macof -i eth0 # The defense (covered in Countermeasures): cap MACs per port # switchport port-security maximum 2

ARP poisoning: the man-in-the-middle workhorse

ARP poisoning (also called ARP spoofing) exploits the authentication flaw described in Fundamentals: hosts accept any ARP reply, even unsolicited ones. The attack redirects traffic between two hosts — typically a victim and the network gateway — through the attacker's machine, giving a true man-in-the-middle position. The mechanism is elegant and worth walking through carefully, because it is the foundation for DNS poisoning, session hijacking, and credential capture.

The attacker sends two forged ARP replies. To the victim, they announce "the gateway's IP is at my MAC address." To the gateway, they announce "the victim's IP is at my MAC address." Both devices update their ARP caches with the poisoned mappings. Now every packet the victim sends toward the internet goes to the attacker, who forwards it to the real gateway (so the victim notices nothing), and every packet coming back for the victim likewise passes through the attacker. The attacker sits invisibly in the middle, able to read, record, or modify everything.

Attacker → Victim:
"gateway = my MAC"
+
Attacker → Gateway:
"victim = my MAC"
=
All victim ↔ internet
traffic flows through attacker

A critical detail: for the victim to notice nothing, the attacker must enable IP forwarding so packets are relayed to their real destination. Without it, the victim simply loses connectivity — a denial of service rather than a stealthy intercept, and a dead giveaway. The classic tools are arpspoof (dsniff), Ettercap (a full MITM framework with a GUI), and Bettercap (the modern, scriptable successor). Ettercap also offers a port stealing mode for environments where static ARP entries make poisoning ineffective.

# Enable forwarding so the victim keeps working (stealthy intercept) echo 1 > /proc/sys/net/ipv4/ip_forward # Poison both directions between victim and gateway arpspoof -i eth0 -t 192.168.1.100 192.168.1.1 # tell victim we're the gateway arpspoof -i eth0 -t 192.168.1.1 192.168.1.100 # tell gateway we're the victim # Or the modern all-in-one: bettercap -iface eth0 -eval "set arp.spoof.targets 192.168.1.100; arp.spoof on; net.sniff on"

DNS poisoning: controlling where names resolve

Once an attacker holds a man-in-the-middle position via ARP poisoning, DNS poisoning becomes a natural next step and a powerful one. DNS translates human-readable names (bank.com) into IP addresses, and like ARP it was designed without authentication of responses. From the MITM position, the attacker intercepts the victim's DNS queries and returns forged answers — pointing bank.com at an attacker-controlled server hosting a convincing phishing replica. The victim's browser shows the correct address they typed, but the traffic goes to the attacker. This is how a sniffing foothold escalates into active credential theft and malware delivery.

DNS poisoning can also occur at other layers — poisoning a resolver's cache, or tampering with the local hosts file on a compromised machine — but the MITM-based version is the one that follows directly from the sniffing techniques in this module. Its most important defenses are DNSSEC, which cryptographically signs DNS records so forged answers are rejected, and DNS over HTTPS/TLS (DoH/DoT), which encrypts queries so a man in the middle cannot read or alter them.

🎯 Advanced Attack Techniques

Beyond the basics

ARP poisoning and MAC flooding are the foundation, but a full sniffing engagement draws on a wider set of techniques that either enable interception in specific circumstances or extend what the attacker can do once positioned in the traffic path. This section covers the most exam-relevant of them: MAC spoofing, DHCP-based attacks, and the layer-2 manipulations that target switch behavior directly.

MAC spoofing: becoming someone else on the wire

MAC spoofing is changing an interface's MAC address to impersonate another device. Because the MAC address is only stored in software when the interface initializes, it is trivial to override — a single command. This simplicity makes it a versatile building block. An attacker uses MAC spoofing to bypass MAC-based access controls (many networks, especially wireless, restrict access to a whitelist of known MAC addresses — which MAC spoofing defeats entirely by cloning an approved one), to evade tracking that ties activity to a hardware address, and to impersonate a trusted device as part of a larger attack.

The key defensive lesson is that MAC address whitelisting alone is not a security control. An attacker who can observe one legitimate MAC on the network — trivial with any sniffing — can clone it and inherit its access. Real port-level security requires 802.1X authentication, not MAC filtering.

# Clone an approved device's MAC to defeat MAC filtering ifconfig eth0 down ifconfig eth0 hw ether 00:11:22:33:44:55 # a MAC observed on the network ifconfig eth0 up # Or with the macchanger utility macchanger -m 00:11:22:33:44:55 eth0

DHCP attacks: starvation and rogue servers

DHCP automatically assigns IP configuration to devices as they join a network, and — following the pattern of every protocol in this module — it trusts responses without authentication. Two attacks exploit this. In DHCP starvation, the attacker floods the legitimate DHCP server with requests using spoofed MAC addresses until its pool of available addresses is exhausted, so real clients can no longer obtain an address — a denial of service. Starvation is often the setup for the second attack: with the real server unable to respond, the attacker stands up a rogue DHCP server of their own.

A rogue DHCP server is powerful because DHCP hands out not just an IP address but the client's default gateway and DNS server. By supplying its own address as the gateway, the rogue server routes all of the victim's traffic through the attacker — achieving a man-in-the-middle position much like ARP poisoning, but at the moment the victim joins the network. By supplying a malicious DNS server, it controls name resolution for every lookup the victim makes. The defense is DHCP snooping, covered in Countermeasures, which designates which switch ports are allowed to send DHCP responses and drops rogue offers on all others.

# DHCP starvation: exhaust the legitimate server's address pool dhcpstarv -i eth0 # or: yersinia -G (GUI for layer-2 attacks including DHCP) # The defense (Countermeasures): trust only the real server's port # ip dhcp snooping # interface g0/1 # ip dhcp snooping trust

Layer-2 manipulation: VLAN hopping and spanning-tree attacks

More advanced attackers target the switch's own control protocols. VLAN hopping defeats the network segmentation that VLANs are supposed to provide — either by switch spoofing (negotiating a trunk link the attacker should not have, gaining access to all VLANs) or double tagging (crafting a frame with two VLAN tags so it leaks from one VLAN into another). STP (Spanning Tree Protocol) attacks inject crafted BPDU messages to convince the network that the attacker's machine should be the root bridge, drawing traffic through it. These are less common in practice than ARP poisoning but appear on the exam and matter in well-segmented enterprise networks. Their defenses are specific switch hardening features — disabling automatic trunk negotiation, BPDU guard, and careful VLAN configuration.

🛠️ Tools & Analysis Techniques

Capture is easy; analysis is the skill

Putting an interface in promiscuous mode and capturing packets takes one command. The real skill — and the reason network analysis is a career in itself — is turning millions of captured packets into meaning: finding the one credential in a sea of traffic, reconstructing a file transfer, or spotting the anomaly that reveals an attack. The tools below fall into two groups: those that capture and dissect traffic (Wireshark, tcpdump), and those that automate an attack workflow (Ettercap, Bettercap). Knowing which to reach for, and how to filter effectively, is what separates a useful analyst from someone drowning in packets.

Wireshark: the analyst's microscope

Wireshark is the definitive graphical packet analyzer. It captures live traffic or opens saved capture files (.pcap), and its power is in dissection: it understands hundreds of protocols and presents each packet as a readable, drill-down tree — Ethernet frame, IP header, TCP segment, and application-layer payload, each expandable. Its most important feature for practical work is display filters, a rich query language that lets you narrow millions of packets to exactly the traffic of interest.

The single most valuable Wireshark skill is Follow TCP Stream, which reassembles all the packets of a single conversation into the actual data exchanged — turning a scatter of TCP segments back into the HTTP request, the email, or the login exchange a human can read. On unencrypted protocols, this is where plaintext credentials appear. Learning a handful of display filters covers most real work:

# Wireshark display filters — the high-value ones http.request.method == "POST" # form submissions (often logins) tcp.port == 21 || tcp.port == 23 # FTP and Telnet (plaintext protocols) ip.addr == 192.168.1.100 # all traffic to/from one host http.authorization # HTTP Basic Auth headers (base64 creds) tcp contains "password" # crude but effective content search

tcpdump: capture anywhere

tcpdump is the command-line counterpart to Wireshark, and it matters because it runs where Wireshark cannot — on a headless server, over SSH, on any minimal system. The common workflow pairs them: capture with tcpdump on a remote host, save to a file, then pull the file back to analyze in Wireshark's GUI. tcpdump uses BPF (Berkeley Packet Filter) capture filters, which are applied at capture time (unlike Wireshark's display filters, applied afterward) — an important distinction, because capture filters reduce what you store, while display filters reduce what you view.

# Capture to a file for later Wireshark analysis tcpdump -i eth0 -w capture.pcap # Capture only HTTP traffic, print readable output tcpdump -i eth0 -A 'tcp port 80' # Capture traffic to/from one host tcpdump -i eth0 host 192.168.1.100

Attack frameworks: Ettercap and Bettercap

Where Wireshark and tcpdump observe, Ettercap and Bettercap act. They automate the full active-sniffing workflow — ARP poisoning, traffic interception, and on-the-fly manipulation — in a single tool. Ettercap is the older, GUI-capable framework with a plugin system; Bettercap is its modern, scriptable successor, designed around a caplet/module system and actively maintained. Both can poison ARP caches, capture the redirected traffic, extract credentials automatically, and even modify traffic in transit (injecting content, downgrading HTTPS where possible). For a penetration tester they compress a multi-step attack into a repeatable script; for a student they are the clearest demonstration of how the individual techniques in this module combine into a complete intercept.

Reconstructing credentials from a capture

The payoff of sniffing is what you can extract, and the analysis tools above make credential recovery straightforward against unencrypted protocols. HTTP Basic Auth transmits credentials as trivially-reversible base64 in a header. FTP and Telnet send usernames and passwords in literal plaintext. Even where a protocol hashes credentials, captured hashes can be cracked offline (as covered in the System Hacking module) or replayed. Specialized tools like dsniff automate this extraction, watching a capture for authentication in dozens of protocols and printing the credentials directly. This is precisely why the countermeasures section's emphasis on encryption is the true last line of defense — it does not stop the capture, but it makes the captured data worthless.

🛡️ Countermeasures & Defense Strategies

Two layers of defense

Defending against sniffing works at two distinct layers, and a strong posture uses both. The first layer is prevention at the switch — configuration features that stop the active-sniffing attacks from succeeding in the first place, so traffic is never redirected to the attacker. The second layer is encryption — the assumption that capture might succeed anyway, and the guarantee that captured traffic is useless. Prevention keeps the attacker out of the traffic path; encryption makes the path worthless if they get in. Relying on only one is a mistake: switch defenses can be misconfigured or bypassed, and encryption does not stop the denial-of-service and traffic-analysis aspects of these attacks.

The switch security stack — and the order it must be built in

Modern managed switches provide a layered set of features that, enabled together, neutralize essentially every active-sniffing attack in this module. The critical exam and real-world point is that these features have dependencies and must be enabled in the right order — DHCP snooping is the foundation the others build on.

1. Port security → stops MAC flooding

Limits how many MAC addresses a switch port will learn (often just one or two). When the limit is exceeded — exactly what MAC flooding does — the port shuts down or drops the excess rather than overflowing the CAM table. This directly defeats the CAM-overflow attack.

2. DHCP snooping → the foundation

Classifies ports as trusted (uplinks, the real DHCP server) or untrusted (clients), and drops DHCP responses arriving on untrusted ports — defeating rogue DHCP servers. Crucially, it also builds a binding table of legitimate IP-to-MAC-to-port mappings that the next two features depend on. Enable this first.

3. Dynamic ARP Inspection → stops ARP poisoning

Intercepts every ARP packet on untrusted ports and validates it against the DHCP snooping binding table. An ARP reply claiming a MAC-to-IP mapping that does not match the table is dropped before it can poison any cache. DAI is the single most effective control against ARP poisoning — but it is useless without DHCP snooping providing its baseline.

4. IP Source Guard → stops IP spoofing

Extends the same binding table to filter IP packets, forwarding only those whose source IP matches the port's binding. This closes off IP spoofing even after other attacks, completing the defense-in-depth stack.

The dependency in one sentence: DHCP snooping builds the trusted binding table; Dynamic ARP Inspection and IP Source Guard both validate against that table — so enabling DAI without DHCP snooping first gives it nothing to check against. For the handful of hosts with static IPs (not in the DHCP table), you add manual ARP ACLs so DAI knows they are legitimate.

# Cisco configuration — note the order ip dhcp snooping # 1. foundation, enable first ip dhcp snooping vlan 10 interface g0/1 ip dhcp snooping trust # trust the uplink / DHCP server port interface g0/2 switchport port-security # 2. cap MACs per client port switchport port-security maximum 2 ip dhcp snooping limit rate 15 # rate-limit to stop starvation ip arp inspection vlan 10 # 3. DAI validates against the binding table ip verify source # 4. IP Source Guard on client ports

Encryption: the defense that assumes capture succeeds

Switch hardening keeps attackers out of the traffic path, but a resilient posture assumes that somewhere, sometime, capture will succeed — a misconfigured switch, an unmanaged segment, a compromised host, or a wireless network. Encryption is what makes that captured traffic worthless. A man in the middle who intercepts a TLS-protected connection sees only ciphertext; the credentials, session tokens, and data are unreadable. This is why the industry-wide shift to HTTPS everywhere has done more to reduce the real-world impact of sniffing than any switch feature.

  • TLS/HTTPS everywhere, enforced with HSTS so browsers refuse to fall back to plaintext HTTP — this defeats credential capture even from a full MITM position.
  • VPNs to tunnel all traffic through an encrypted channel on untrusted networks (public Wi-Fi being the canonical case).
  • SSH instead of Telnet, SFTP instead of FTP — replacing the legacy plaintext protocols that make sniffing so productive.
  • DNSSEC and DNS over HTTPS/TLS to authenticate and encrypt name resolution, defeating DNS poisoning.

Detection: catching active sniffing

Here is the defensive silver lining that ties the module together: because the attacks that work on modern networks are active, they leave traces and can be detected. Passive sniffing on a switch sees almost nothing, so the attacks an adversary must actually use all inject packets. arpwatch and similar tools monitor for the tell-tale signs of ARP poisoning — an IP address suddenly mapping to a new MAC, or a single MAC claiming many IPs. Feeding ARP and DHCP logs into a SIEM lets defenders alert on these anomalies. Intrusion detection systems carry signatures for MAC flooding, ARP spoofing, and rogue DHCP activity. The lesson to leave the module with: on a modern network, a sniffing attacker who wants to see your traffic has to make noise to do it — and noise can be caught.
