CEH v13 Module 10: Denial-of-Service

Denial-of-Service and Distributed Denial-of-Service

Volumetric, protocol, and application-layer attacks; botnet architecture; and the detection, rate-limiting, and scrubbing strategies used to absorb them.

🎯 Module Overview

What denial of service is — and why it's different

Most attacks in this course aim at confidentiality (stealing data) or integrity (altering it). Denial-of-service attacks target the third pillar of security: availability. The goal is not to break in or steal anything, but to make a system, service, or network unusable for its legitimate users. A successful DoS attack does not compromise a single account or exfiltrate a single file — yet it can cost an organization millions in lost revenue, drive customers away, and, increasingly, serve as a smokescreen for a second attack happening in the noise.

The mechanism is conceptually simple: every system has finite resources — bandwidth, memory, CPU, connection slots, application worker threads — and a DoS attack consumes one of those resources faster than legitimate traffic can, until nothing is left for real users. The art of the attack is in finding the cheapest way to exhaust the most expensive resource; the art of the defense is in absorbing or filtering the malicious load before it reaches that limit.

DoS vs. DDoS: the force multiplier

The distinction between the two acronyms is the single most important concept in this module. A DoS (Denial of Service) attack comes from a single source — one machine flooding a target. This is easy to defend against: the target simply blocks the offending IP address, and the attack stops. A DDoS (Distributed Denial of Service) attack comes from thousands or millions of sources simultaneously, coordinated by an attacker who controls them all. This distribution is what makes DDoS so formidable: there is no single IP to block, the aggregate traffic can dwarf any target's capacity, and the sources are often innocent, compromised third-party devices whose owners have no idea they are participating.

DoS — one source

A single machine attacks the target. Blockable by dropping one IP. Limited by the attacker's own bandwidth. Largely a historical or niche threat today, useful mainly for understanding the underlying techniques.

DDoS — many sources

A coordinated army of compromised machines (a botnet) attacks at once. No single IP to block; aggregate bandwidth can reach tens of terabits per second. The dominant real-world form, and the reason cloud-scale mitigation exists.

The scale problem in 2026

To understand why DDoS defense has become a specialized, cloud-scale discipline, consider how the numbers have escalated. The Mirai botnet of 2016 — the first to weaponize insecure IoT devices at scale — took down major sites with attacks around 1 Tbps and shocked the industry. By late 2025, the record had climbed to 31.4 Tbps, attributed to the Aisuru-Kimwolf botnet of an estimated one to four million compromised devices. Hyper-volumetric attacks exceeding 1 Tbps, once a once-a-year headline, are now measured in the dozens per day. Perhaps most significantly, chunks of these botnets are rented out as a service for a few hundred dollars, putting attack capacity that dwarfs most organizations' entire internet connection within reach of an unskilled attacker.

The lesson this module builds toward is that no single organization can absorb a modern hyper-volumetric attack on its own connection — the defense has moved into the cloud, to providers whose networks are large enough to soak up terabits of malicious traffic and scrub it before it ever reaches the target.

Authorized testing only. Launching a denial-of-service attack against any system you do not own is a serious crime in virtually every jurisdiction, and the distributed, third-party nature of DDoS means you would also be misusing the compromised devices of innocent parties. Even stress-testing your own infrastructure can spill over onto shared hosting or upstream providers, so authorized DoS testing is done only in isolated lab environments or with explicit written permission from every affected party.

💡 DoS/DDoS Fundamentals

The three categories that organize everything

Every DoS/DDoS attack, no matter how it is marketed or named, works by exhausting one of three kinds of resource. Learning these three categories is the key that unlocks the whole subject, because each category attacks a different layer, requires a different defense, and produces a different signature. The CEH exam and every serious mitigation product organize their thinking around exactly this taxonomy.

1. Volumetric attacks

Exhaust bandwidth — the size of the pipe. The attacker sends more data than the target's internet connection can carry, so legitimate traffic cannot get through, like a highway jammed with cars. Measured in bits per second (bps). This is the "brute force" category and the source of the terabit-scale headlines.

2. Protocol attacks

Exhaust connection-state resources — the finite tables in servers, firewalls, and load balancers that track connections. The attacker abuses how a protocol works (classically TCP) to consume these slots. Measured in packets per second (pps). Efficient: a small amount of traffic can exhaust a large amount of state.

3. Application-layer attacks

Exhaust application resources — the CPU, memory, and worker threads a server spends processing each request. The attacker sends requests that look legitimate but are deliberately expensive to serve. Measured in requests per second (rps). Low-bandwidth, hard to distinguish from real traffic, and increasingly the weapon of choice.

Why the measurement units matter

The three different units — bps, pps, rps — are not trivia; they tell you what a given attack is actually stressing and therefore how to defend. A volumetric attack quoted at "7.3 Tbps" is about the sheer volume of bits saturating a link, defended by having a bigger pipe or scrubbing upstream. A protocol attack quoted in "packets per second" is about the rate of packets exhausting state tables, defended by devices that track and validate connection state. An application attack quoted in "requests per second" — like the 200-million-rps HTTP floods of late 2025 — is about the number of expensive operations demanded of the application, defended by rate limiting and behavioral analysis at Layer 7. When you read a threat report, the unit tells you which category you are looking at.

The anatomy of a DDoS: the attacker's hierarchy

A distributed attack is not a mob of individuals; it is a command hierarchy. At the top is the attacker (sometimes called the botmaster), who never sends attack traffic directly. Below them are one or more command-and-control (C2) servers, which relay the attacker's instructions. At the bottom are the bots or zombies — the compromised devices that actually generate the flood, collectively a botnet. This structure serves two purposes: it multiplies the attacker's firepower to the combined bandwidth of the entire botnet, and it insulates the attacker, whose own machine never touches the victim, making attribution extremely difficult.

Understanding this hierarchy is also what reveals the defensive opportunities: disrupting the C2 servers can decapitate an entire botnet at once (as when Lumen's researchers disrupted 550+ Aisuru C2 nodes in October 2025), and securing the IoT devices that become bots shrinks the pool of available zombies. The botnet section explores this structure in depth.

Reflection and amplification: getting others to attack for you

The most bandwidth-efficient volumetric attacks use a devastating trick called reflection with amplification. The attacker sends a small request to a third-party server (a DNS resolver, an NTP server, a Memcached server) but spoofs the source address so the reply is sent to the victim instead of back to the attacker. This is reflection — the third party unwittingly reflects traffic at the target. Amplification is what makes it dangerous: for certain protocols, the reply is far larger than the request. A small DNS query produces a much larger response; Memcached, the most extreme case, can amplify traffic by a factor of 51,200×. The attacker spends a trickle of bandwidth and directs a torrent at the victim, from thousands of innocent reflectors whose addresses are what the victim sees.

Why amplification exists: these are UDP-based protocols with no handshake, so a server cannot verify that the requester's address is genuine before replying. The defense is twofold — network operators implementing source-address validation (BCP 38) so spoofed packets can't leave their networks in the first place, and administrators securing or restricting the reflector services (open DNS resolvers, exposed Memcached) that make amplification possible.

🎯 Attack Types

The classic attacks that define each category

Each of the three categories from the Fundamentals section has canonical attacks that the exam expects you to know by name and mechanism. Understanding how each one exhausts its target resource — not just its name — is what lets you recognize a novel variant as a member of a familiar family.

Protocol attacks: the SYN flood

The SYN flood is the textbook protocol attack, and it beautifully illustrates how a small amount of traffic can exhaust a large resource by abusing how a protocol is supposed to work. Recall the TCP three-way handshake: a client sends SYN, the server replies SYN-ACK and reserves resources for the pending connection, and the client completes it with ACK. The SYN flood exploits the gap: the attacker sends a torrent of SYN packets, usually with spoofed source addresses, but never sends the final ACK. The server dutifully allocates connection state for each one and waits — holding these half-open connections until they time out. With its connection table full of half-open entries, the server can accept no new legitimate connections. A trickle of SYN packets ties up a server's entire capacity to accept clients.

# A SYN flood with hping3 (authorized lab use only) hping3 -S --flood -p 80 --rand-source 192.168.1.10 # -S send SYN packets # --flood send as fast as possible # --rand-source spoof random source IPs (defeats simple IP blocking)

The defense is SYN cookies, a clever technique where the server encodes the connection state into the sequence number of its SYN-ACK rather than storing it, so it allocates no memory until the handshake actually completes. A flood of SYNs that never complete therefore costs the server nothing.

Volumetric attacks: floods and amplification

Volumetric attacks are conceptually the simplest — send more traffic than the pipe can hold — but their scale is what makes them fearsome. UDP floods blast the target with UDP packets to random ports; because UDP is connectionless, the target wastes resources checking for (nonexistent) listening applications and replying with "port unreachable" messages. ICMP floods (ping floods) do the same with ICMP echo requests. Neither is efficient on its own — the attacker must supply all the bandwidth — which is why serious volumetric attacks use the reflection and amplification technique from the Fundamentals section, borrowing the bandwidth of thousands of innocent DNS, NTP, or Memcached servers to multiply a small attack into a terabit-scale flood.

Application-layer attacks: cheap requests, expensive answers

Application-layer (Layer 7) attacks are the most sophisticated and increasingly the most common, because they turn the server's own logic against it and are extremely hard to distinguish from legitimate users. Instead of brute-force volume, they send requests that are individually valid but deliberately expensive to serve.

HTTP flood

A barrage of seemingly legitimate HTTP GET or POST requests, ideally aimed at the most expensive operations — a search query, a database-heavy report, a login. Each request looks like a real user, so simple filtering fails; the server drowns in genuine-looking work. Modern versions reach hundreds of millions of requests per second.

Slowloris (low-and-slow)

The opposite of a flood: it opens many connections and holds them open by sending partial HTTP requests very slowly, never quite finishing. It ties up the server's finite connection pool with almost no bandwidth — a single machine can take down a web server. Defeated by connection timeouts and reverse proxies that buffer complete requests.

A modern and important example is HTTP/2 Rapid Reset (CVE-2023-44487), which exploited a feature of the HTTP/2 protocol allowing a client to cancel a request immediately after sending it. By automating this request-then-cancel pattern, attackers forced servers to do the setup work for millions of requests per second while the connections themselves were instantly reset — powering the record-breaking 398-million-rps attack Google disclosed in 2023. It is a perfect illustration of the application-layer principle: not volume, but abuse of protocol logic to make the server spend resources cheaply demanded.

The trade-off, summarized

The three categories sit on a spectrum. Volumetric attacks are loud, powerful, and blockable — enormous but obvious, and stoppable by upstream scrubbing. Application-layer attacks are quiet, efficient, and evasive — small enough to slip past volume-based defenses and hard to separate from real users, requiring behavioral analysis to catch. Protocol attacks sit in between. This is why no single defense suffices, and why the countermeasures section describes a layered stack rather than one silver bullet.

🤖 Botnets

The engine behind every large DDoS

A botnet is a network of compromised, remotely-controlled devices — the army that makes distributed attacks possible. Each device, called a bot or zombie, runs hidden malware that awaits commands from the attacker's command-and-control infrastructure. The owners of these devices are almost always unaware: their home router, security camera, or smart TV is quietly participating in attacks while functioning normally for them. Botnets are the reason a single attacker can command tens of terabits of bandwidth, and understanding their lifecycle reveals where they can be disrupted.

How a botnet is built and controlled

A botnet's life follows a predictable cycle. First comes infection: the malware spreads by scanning the internet for vulnerable devices — most notoriously IoT devices with default or weak credentials — and installing itself. Next is rallying: each newly infected bot phones home to the command-and-control infrastructure to register itself and await orders. Then comes the attack: the botmaster issues a command through C2, and every bot simultaneously floods the designated target. Between attacks, the botnet maintains and grows, continuously scanning for new victims and updating its malware.

The command-and-control architecture is the botnet's nervous system and its vulnerability. Centralized C2 — where all bots connect to a small set of servers (often over IRC or HTTP) — is simple and responsive but creates a single point of failure: take down the C2 servers and the whole botnet goes dark. This is exactly what happened when researchers disrupted 550+ Aisuru command nodes in late 2025. To resist this, sophisticated botnets use peer-to-peer C2, where bots relay commands among themselves with no central server to seize, making the botnet far more resilient but harder for the attacker to control.

Mirai and its descendants: the IoT era

No botnet is more important to understand than Mirai, because it defined the modern era and its code underpins most of what came after. In 2016, Mirai demonstrated that the explosion of insecure IoT devices — cameras, routers, DVRs shipped with default passwords and never patched — was a vast, untapped botnet reservoir. It spread by trying a short list of default credentials against internet-connected devices, assembled hundreds of thousands of them, and launched attacks around 1 Tbps that took down security journalist Brian Krebs' site, the hosting provider OVH, and the DNS provider Dyn — the last of which cascaded into outages at Netflix, Twitter, and PayPal. Crucially, Mirai's source code was published, and every major IoT botnet since is a descendant or imitator.

The current apex is Aisuru (also called Kimwolf), a Mirai-class botnet of an estimated one to four million devices — primarily Android TVs and unpatched residential routers — that built on Mirai's architecture with better evasion and higher packet throughput. Aisuru was responsible for the record 31.4 Tbps attack of November 2025 and over a thousand hyper-volumetric attacks in a single quarter. Its rise illustrates two trends the module keeps returning to: attacks keep getting larger, and botnet capacity is increasingly rented out as a commodity service.

DDoS-as-a-service: the commoditization of firepower

Perhaps the most consequential development is that launching a devastating DDoS no longer requires building a botnet. "Booter" and "stresser" services — marketed with a thin veneer of legitimacy as network-testing tools — rent out botnet capacity for a few dollars to a few hundred dollars. Chunks of the Aisuru botnet have reportedly been offered this way, putting hyper-volumetric attack capability that exceeds most organizations' entire internet connection into the hands of anyone with a credit card and a grievance. This commoditization is why DDoS has shifted from a rare, sophisticated threat to a routine one, and why defense has had to industrialize in response.

Where botnets can be broken

The botnet lifecycle exposes three points of defensive leverage, and a complete strategy touches all three. Shrink the pool by securing the devices that become bots — changing default IoT credentials, applying firmware updates, and segmenting IoT devices onto isolated networks. Decapitate the control by identifying and disrupting C2 infrastructure, a job usually done by security researchers, ISPs, and law enforcement working together. Absorb the output with the cloud-scale scrubbing described in Countermeasures, which handles the attack traffic even when the botnet itself cannot be dismantled. Because no one of these is sufficient alone — you cannot secure every IoT device, seize every C2, or out-scale every botnet — real defense combines all three.

🛠️ Attack Tools

Understanding the tools reveals the attacks

The tools used to generate DoS traffic are worth studying not to wield them, but because each one embodies a technique from the previous sections, and recognizing a tool's signature is part of detection. They range from crude flooding utilities that anyone can point and click, to precise packet crafters used by professionals for authorized testing. A defender who knows what these tools produce knows what to watch for in their traffic.

Volumetric flooding tools

LOIC (Low Orbit Ion Cannon)

A simple, open-source flooding tool that gained infamy in hacktivist campaigns because it let non-technical volunteers point their machines at a target with a single click, coordinating a crude voluntary botnet. It offers no anonymity — participants' real IP addresses are exposed in the flood — which is how many early users were identified and prosecuted. Its significance is historical and social more than technical.

HOIC (High Orbit Ion Cannon)

LOIC's successor, focused on HTTP flooding with "booster" scripts that randomized requests to evade simple filtering and could target multiple sites at once. It represented the shift from crude packet floods toward application-layer attacks that are harder to distinguish from real traffic.

Low-and-slow tools

Slowloris is the canonical low-and-slow tool, and it upends the intuition that DoS requires massive bandwidth. It opens many connections to a web server and keeps each alive by trickling out partial HTTP headers, never completing a request. The server holds every connection open waiting for the rest, and its finite connection pool fills with these deliberate stragglers — all from a single machine using almost no bandwidth. RUDY (R-U-Dead-Yet) applies the same philosophy to HTTP POST bodies, submitting form data one byte at a time. These tools are a favorite teaching example because they prove that DoS is about exhausting a resource — here, connection slots — not about raw volume.

# Slowloris ties up a server's connection pool with partial requests slowloris 192.168.1.10 -p 80 -s 500 # -s 500 open 500 slow connections # Defense: connection timeouts + a reverse proxy that buffers full requests

Packet crafting: hping3 and its uses

hping3 is not a DoS tool per se but a general-purpose packet crafter, which is precisely why it is valuable for authorized testing: it can construct almost any packet by hand, letting a tester reproduce a SYN flood, a UDP flood, or a custom protocol attack with exact control over every field. Its --rand-source option spoofs source addresses to simulate a distributed attack from a single machine. Because it operates at the packet level, hping3 is the tool of choice for validating that a defense actually works — a defender can generate a controlled SYN flood in the lab and confirm that SYN cookies hold.

# Controlled SYN flood for authorized defense testing hping3 -S --flood -p 80 --rand-source target.lab # UDP flood variant hping3 --udp --flood -p 53 target.lab

From tools to services

The trajectory of these tools tells the story of the whole field. Early tools like LOIC required each participant to run software and expose themselves. Botnet malware removed the need for volunteers by conscripting unwitting devices. And DDoS-as-a-service removed the need for the attacker to build anything at all — the booter services described in the Botnets section package this entire capability behind a web form. The defensive implication is sobering: the skill required to launch a serious attack has collapsed to nearly zero, which is why detection and cloud-scale mitigation, not deterrence, carry the defensive burden.

🔍 Detection Techniques

The core challenge: attack traffic that looks legitimate

Detecting a DDoS attack sounds trivial — surely a flood is obvious? — but the real difficulty is the same one that runs through this entire module: the best attacks are engineered to resemble legitimate traffic. A volumetric flood is easy to spot but hard to stop; an application-layer attack of genuine-looking HTTP requests is hard even to identify, because each request is individually valid. Detection is therefore about distinguishing malicious patterns from legitimate ones fast enough to react, and doing it without blocking real users. The faster and more accurately you detect, the smaller the window of disruption.

The two detection philosophies

Signature-based detection

Matches traffic against known attack patterns — the specific packet structures of a SYN flood, the fingerprint of a particular tool, the request signature of HTTP/2 Rapid Reset. Fast and accurate for known attacks, and produces few false positives. Its weakness is that it cannot catch a novel attack it has no signature for, so it must be constantly updated.

Anomaly-based detection

Learns what normal traffic looks like — typical volume, packet rates, request distributions, geographic sources — and flags significant deviations. This can catch never-before-seen attacks, which is its great strength. Its weakness is false positives: a legitimate traffic spike (a product launch, a viral moment) can look like an attack, so it requires careful tuning and a well-established baseline.

Mature defenses use both: signatures for the fast, confident blocking of known attacks, and anomaly detection as the safety net for the unknown. Increasingly, the anomaly side is powered by machine learning that models normal behavior in far more dimensions than a human could tune by hand — essential against AI-enhanced Layer 7 attacks that deliberately mimic human users.

What to watch: the indicators

Regardless of philosophy, certain signals reliably indicate a possible DoS attack, and knowing them is practical detection knowledge:

  • A sudden, sustained traffic spike far above baseline — the clearest volumetric indicator, though it must be distinguished from legitimate surges.
  • A flood of traffic from a single source or a narrow range — points to a simple DoS rather than a distributed one, and is easily blocked.
  • An abnormal protocol or packet-size distribution — a spike in SYN packets without corresponding ACKs signals a SYN flood; a flood of identically-sized UDP packets suggests a reflection attack.
  • Requests concentrated on one expensive endpoint — many requests hammering a search or login URL, the signature of an application-layer flood.
  • Many slow, incomplete connections — the fingerprint of a Slowloris-style low-and-slow attack.
  • Geographic or behavioral anomalies — traffic suddenly arriving from regions or at times that do not match your normal user base.

Where detection happens

Detection is deployed at several vantage points, each seeing a different slice of the problem. Network flow analysis (using NetFlow/sFlow data from routers) gives a broad, real-time view of traffic volumes and sources across the whole network — ideal for spotting volumetric attacks early. Intrusion detection systems (IDS) inspect packets for attack signatures. Application and server monitoring watches resource exhaustion — CPU, memory, connection counts, response times — which often reveals an application-layer attack that never showed up as unusual volume. And upstream provider monitoring, at the ISP or cloud-mitigation level, sees attacks building before they even reach the target. The most robust posture correlates signals from all of these, because a sophisticated attack may only be visible when the broad network view and the application-resource view are considered together.

🛡️ Countermeasures

Why you can't defend a modern DDoS alone

The single most important lesson of this module's defensive half is that no organization can absorb a hyper-volumetric attack on its own infrastructure. If an attacker can direct 31 terabits per second at you and your internet connection is measured in gigabits, no firewall, no server tuning, and no amount of on-premises hardware can help — the traffic saturates your link before it ever reaches your equipment. This physical reality has reshaped DDoS defense from an on-premises problem into a cloud-scale one, and it explains why the countermeasures below build from local hardening up to distributed, cloud-based absorption.

The layered defense stack

1. Local hardening (necessary, not sufficient)

Configuration that raises your own resilience: SYN cookies to defeat SYN floods without allocating memory, rate limiting to cap requests per source, connection timeouts to defeat low-and-slow attacks, and tuned firewall rules. This handles small attacks and buys time, but a large volumetric attack overwhelms it — it is the floor, not the ceiling, of defense.

2. Overprovisioning and load balancing

Having more capacity than you normally need, and spreading load across many servers, so the target is harder to exhaust. Useful headroom against modest attacks, but economically impossible to scale to terabit attacks — you cannot buy 31 Tbps of standing capacity for a normal service.

3. Cloud scrubbing services

The essential layer for serious protection. Providers like Cloudflare, Akamai, and AWS Shield route your traffic through their globally-distributed networks, which are large enough to absorb terabits of attack traffic, scrub out the malicious portion, and forward only clean traffic to you. Their scale is the only thing that matches the attackers' scale.

4. Web application firewall (WAF)

Specifically for application-layer attacks that slip past volume-based defenses. A WAF inspects HTTP requests and applies behavioral analysis to separate genuine users from a flood of legitimate-looking malicious requests — the only effective answer to sophisticated Layer 7 attacks.

How cloud scrubbing works

Cloud DDoS mitigation is worth understanding in a little depth because it is now the backbone of real-world defense. The provider positions itself between the internet and your servers — typically by having your DNS point at their network, or via BGP routing that draws your traffic through their scrubbing centers. Because these providers operate anycast networks with data centers worldwide, an incoming attack is automatically distributed across their entire global capacity rather than concentrated on one link. Each scrubbing center inspects the traffic, drops what its detection systems classify as malicious, and passes clean traffic to your origin. The largest providers absorb and filter attacks measured in tens of terabits per second automatically, which is exactly how the record 31.4 Tbps attack was mitigated in seconds. For most organizations, subscribing to such a service is the single most effective DDoS decision they can make.

The strategic point: DDoS defense has become a matter of leverage rather than local strength. You cannot win a bandwidth contest against a million-device botnet — but you can stand behind a provider whose network is bigger than the botnet. The defense scales by borrowing scale, the mirror image of how the attack scales by borrowing devices.

Preparation: the incident response plan

Technology is only half of readiness. Because a DDoS attack is a crisis that unfolds in minutes, organizations that weather them well have prepared in advance: a written DDoS response plan naming who does what, pre-established contacts with their ISP and mitigation provider, the ability to reroute traffic quickly, and runbooks rehearsed before the attack rather than improvised during it. A recurring theme in post-incident analysis is that the organizations that recovered fastest were not those with the most hardware, but those who had practiced. And because DDoS is increasingly used as a smokescreen for a simultaneous intrusion, the response plan should include watching for other malicious activity while attention is consumed by the flood.

📚 Real-World Case Studies

Learning from the record books

The history of major DDoS attacks is a history of escalation, and each landmark attack introduced a technique or crossed a threshold that changed how defenders think. Studying them chronologically shows the arms race in motion: each record-breaker forced a defensive response, which attackers then worked to overcome. The following cases are the ones every security professional should know.

2016 — Mirai and the Dyn attack: the IoT wake-up call

The Mirai botnet attacks of 2016 are the most historically important in this list because they proved a new category of threat. By conscripting hundreds of thousands of insecure IoT devices — cameras, routers, DVRs with default passwords — Mirai launched roughly 1 Tbps attacks that were unprecedented at the time. The attack on DNS provider Dyn was especially instructive: rather than attack popular sites directly, it took down the DNS infrastructure they depended on, cascading into outages at Netflix, Twitter, Reddit, and PayPal. The lessons were twofold and lasting: the ocean of insecure IoT devices is a strategic vulnerability for the entire internet, and attacking shared infrastructure (like DNS) multiplies impact far beyond the direct target. Mirai's source code was published, seeding every major botnet since.

2018 — GitHub and Memcached amplification: efficiency over volume

In 2018, GitHub was hit by what was then the largest recorded attack, peaking at 1.35 Tbps — but the remarkable part was how. The attackers used Memcached amplification, abusing thousands of exposed Memcached servers that would reply to a tiny spoofed request with an enormous response, achieving amplification factors up to 51,200×. It demonstrated that attackers did not need to build a massive botnet if they could borrow the bandwidth of misconfigured internet infrastructure. The equally instructive part was the defense: GitHub's cloud mitigation provider absorbed and scrubbed the attack in under ten minutes, a preview of the cloud-scale defense that is now standard. The lasting lesson: exposed, unauthenticated UDP services are dangerous reflectors, and securing them is a shared responsibility of the whole internet.

2023 — HTTP/2 Rapid Reset: a protocol flaw at scale

In 2023, Google, Cloudflare, and AWS jointly disclosed a novel application-layer technique, HTTP/2 Rapid Reset (CVE-2023-44487), that powered an attack peaking at 398 million requests per second — far beyond any prior Layer 7 attack. Rather than volume, it exploited a design feature of the HTTP/2 protocol: a client could open a request and immediately cancel it, and by automating this at scale, attackers forced servers to perform the expensive setup for millions of requests per second while spending almost nothing themselves. It was a landmark because it showed that a flaw in a widely-deployed protocol could instantly become a record-breaking weapon, requiring coordinated patching across the entire industry. The lesson: application-layer attacks, not raw volume, represent the sophisticated frontier.

2025 — Aisuru and the 31.4 Tbps era: the new normal

By late 2025, the Aisuru-Kimwolf botnet — a Mirai descendant of one to four million devices — had driven the record to 31.4 Tbps, in an attack that lasted just 35 seconds and was mitigated automatically. What makes this era distinct is not only the raw size but its normalization: hyper-volumetric attacks exceeding 1 Tbps, once a yearly headline, now occur dozens of times a day, and botnet capacity is rented out cheaply as a service. The defining lesson of the current era is that scale has become routine and defense has fully industrialized — the attacks are now mitigated automatically by cloud providers whose networks exceed the botnets' capacity, because human-speed response is far too slow for a 35-second attack.

The through-line

Across a decade, three patterns hold. Attacks escalate relentlessly — from 1 Tbps to 31 Tbps in nine years — so defenses sized for today's attacks are undersized for tomorrow's. Efficiency innovations matter as much as scale — Memcached amplification and HTTP/2 Rapid Reset each broke records not with bigger botnets but with cleverer abuse of existing infrastructure. And defense has moved to the cloud and to automation, because the scale and speed of modern attacks have outrun any on-premises or human-speed response. These are the enduring conclusions to carry out of the module.
