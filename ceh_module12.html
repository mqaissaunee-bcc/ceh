CEH v13 Module 12: Evading IDS, Firewalls, and Honeypots

Evading IDS, Firewalls, and Honeypots

The cat-and-mouse of stealth: how intrusion detection, firewalls, and honeypots work; the fragmentation, encoding, tunneling, and obfuscation used to slip past them; honeypot detection; and the layered detection that counters evasion.

🥷 Overview

The cat-and-mouse game at the perimeter

Real networks are not undefended. Between an attacker and their target stand a set of security systems whose entire purpose is to detect, block, or study intrusions: intrusion detection and prevention systems that watch traffic for signs of attack, firewalls that filter what is allowed through, and honeypots that lie in wait as decoys. This module is about the perpetual contest between these defenses and the attackers who try to slip past them — a genuine cat-and-mouse game in which each side continually adapts to the other. Understanding it is essential for both roles: an attacker who ignores these systems gets caught, and a defender who doesn't understand evasion can't tune their defenses to catch a real adversary.

For the ethical hacker, this knowledge is doubly important. During an authorized engagement, you may need to test whether an organization's defenses actually detect an intrusion — which means understanding how a real attacker would try to evade them. And in the defensive half of the job, understanding evasion is exactly what lets you configure IDS, firewalls, and honeypots to catch the stealthy techniques a skilled adversary would use rather than only the obvious ones.

Three defensive systems, three roles

The module is built around three complementary defenses, each playing a distinct role in protecting a network:

🚨 IDS / IPS

The watchers. An IDS monitors traffic and raises alerts when it spots suspicious activity; an IPS goes further and actively blocks it. They are the systems that decide whether an attack is noticed.

🧱 Firewalls

The gatekeepers. Firewalls sit between trusted and untrusted networks and decide what traffic is allowed through based on rules. They are the barrier an attacker must get past to reach the target at all.

🍯 Honeypots

The traps. Decoy systems that exist only to be attacked, so any interaction with them reveals an intruder. They turn the attacker's own reconnaissance against them, detecting and studying threats.

Together these form the core of perimeter and network defense. An attacker must avoid tripping the watchers, get past the gatekeepers, and steer clear of the traps — all at once.

The unifying logic of evasion

Every evasion technique in this module, however varied, rests on a single principle: defenses can only stop what they can recognize. A signature-based detector only catches attacks matching a known pattern; a firewall only blocks traffic its rules describe; a honeypot only helps if the attacker doesn't realize it's fake. Evasion, therefore, is the art of making malicious activity unrecognizable to these systems — disguising it, splitting it, encrypting it, or hiding it inside traffic the defense trusts. Once you grasp that everything reduces to defeating recognition, the specific techniques become variations on a theme rather than a list to memorize.

What this module covers

We start by understanding the defenses themselves, because you cannot evade what you don't understand: IDS and IPS (how they detect, and their limitations) and firewalls (their types and how each inspects traffic). Then we turn to the offensive core — the evasion techniques that defeat detection and filtering, from packet fragmentation to tunneling to obfuscation. We examine honeypots — how they work, their types, and how attackers try to detect and avoid them — and close with countermeasures: how defenders counter evasion and why layered defense is the answer. This module builds directly on the scan-evasion introduced in Module 3 (Scanning), extending those ideas from evading a port scan to evading a full intrusion.

Evasion is for authorized testing only. Deliberately bypassing an organization's security systems is exactly the kind of activity computer-crime laws prohibit without permission. These techniques are taught so that, under an authorized engagement, you can honestly assess whether defenses work — and so you can build better ones. Use them only within a sanctioned scope and in lab environments.

🚨 IDS & IPS

The watchers: detection vs. prevention

An Intrusion Detection System (IDS) monitors network traffic and system activity, looking for signs of an attack, and raises an alert when it finds them. The crucial thing to understand is that a classic IDS is passive: it detects and reports, but it cannot stop an attack on its own — it is a burglar alarm, not a lock. An Intrusion Prevention System (IPS) takes the next step: it does everything an IDS does and can actively respond, blocking malicious traffic, dropping packets, or terminating the offending connection. This passive-versus-active distinction is the single most tested concept about these systems, and it has a practical consequence: an IPS must sit in-line (all traffic passes through it) so it can block, whereas an IDS can watch a copy of the traffic from the side.

Where they watch: network vs. host

Both IDS and IPS come in two deployment flavors, distinguished by what they monitor. A network-based system (NIDS/NIPS) watches traffic flowing across the network, positioned to see packets to and from many hosts — good for catching attacks in transit. A host-based system (HIDS/HIPS) runs on an individual machine and watches that host's own activity — its files, processes, logs, and local traffic — good for catching attacks that have reached or originated on the endpoint. Mature defenses use both: the network sensor sees the attack coming, the host sensor sees what it does after it lands. This mirrors the two vantage points a defender needs.

How they detect: the two core methods

Understanding how these systems decide something is malicious is the key to understanding how attackers evade them. There are two primary approaches, and each has a characteristic strength and weakness that evasion exploits.

🔖 Signature-based detection

Matches traffic against a database of known attack patterns ("signatures"), much like antivirus. Strength: highly accurate and low false-positives for known attacks. Weakness — the one attackers exploit: it can only catch attacks it already has a signature for. Anything novel, or an old attack altered enough to no longer match, sails through.

📊 Anomaly-based detection

Learns a baseline of "normal" behavior and flags deviations from it. Strength: can catch previously-unknown (zero-day) attacks that no signature covers. Weakness: defining "normal" is hard, so it generates more false positives, and a patient attacker can sometimes stay within the bounds of normal or slowly shift the baseline.

A third method, stateful protocol analysis, compares observed traffic against known-good profiles of how protocols should behave, catching misuse that signatures might miss. Modern systems increasingly layer AI/ML on top of anomaly detection to catch subtle patterns — a development the countermeasures section returns to.

The limitations that create openings

Every one of these systems has inherent limitations, and evasion lives in the gaps between them. Signature detection is blind to what isn't in its database. Anomaly detection can be lulled or overwhelmed. Both struggle with encrypted traffic — a detector that can't read the payload can't match a signature against it, which is why so much modern malware communicates over encrypted channels. Both can be defeated by fragmentation, if the device inspects packets individually without reassembling them the way the target host will. And a passive IDS, by definition, only alerts — if no one acts on the alert quickly, detection without response is little better than no detection at all. These are not implementation bugs; they are structural properties, and the next sections show exactly how attackers turn each into an opening. Knowing them is also what tells a defender where to reinforce.

🧱 Firewalls

The gatekeeper between trusted and untrusted

A firewall is a barrier that sits between a trusted network (your internal systems) and an untrusted one (the internet), controlling which traffic is allowed to pass based on a set of security rules. It is the most fundamental network defense — the gatekeeper that decides what gets in and out. But "firewall" covers a wide range of sophistication, from a simple rule-checker to a deep-inspecting security platform, and the type of firewall determines both what it can catch and how an attacker might get past it. Understanding the types, from simplest to most advanced, is essential to understanding firewall evasion.

The four types, in order of sophistication

1️⃣ Packet-filtering firewall

The simplest: examines each packet in isolation against rules based on source/destination IP, port, and protocol, then allows or drops it. Fast and cheap, but stateless — it has no memory of connections, so it can't tell whether a packet belongs to a legitimate ongoing session. This blind spot is exactly what several evasion techniques exploit.

2️⃣ Stateful inspection firewall

Tracks the state of active connections in a table, so it knows whether a packet is part of an established, legitimate session or an unsolicited probe. This defeats many tricks that fool stateless filters — for instance, it can recognize that an ACK packet doesn't belong to any real connection. The long-standing workhorse of network firewalls.

3️⃣ Application-level (proxy) firewall

Operates at the application layer, inspecting the actual content of specific protocols (like HTTP) rather than just headers. As a proxy, it terminates the connection and relays it, giving deep visibility into what the traffic is really doing — but at a performance cost, and only for the protocols it understands.

4️⃣ Next-generation firewall (NGFW)

The modern standard: combines stateful inspection with deep packet inspection, application awareness (identifying apps regardless of port), integrated IPS, threat intelligence, and often TLS inspection. An NGFW blurs the line between firewall and IDS/IPS, making evasion considerably harder than against older types.

What firewalls can — and can't — do

A firewall's job is access control: enforcing which connections are permitted. But it is not a complete defense, and its limits shape how attackers approach it. A firewall generally must allow some traffic — no organization can function with everything blocked, so ports like 80 and 443 (web) are almost always open. That permitted traffic is precisely the avenue attackers exploit: if HTTP is allowed, hide the attack in HTTP. Simpler firewalls that don't track state or inspect payloads have larger blind spots — they may wave through fragmented packets, unusual flag combinations, or malicious content buried in an allowed protocol. And a firewall can be undermined by misconfiguration, which is one of the most common real-world weaknesses: an overly-permissive rule, a forgotten exception, or a management interface exposed to the internet.

The attacker's mental model

To an attacker, a firewall poses a simple question: what is this thing willing to let through, and can I disguise my traffic as that? The answer depends on the firewall's type and configuration, which is why reconnaissance and scanning (Modules 2–3) matter here — an attacker probes to learn which ports are open, which protocols pass, and whether the firewall tracks state. The ACK scan from the scanning module, for example, exists specifically to map whether a stateful firewall sits in front of a port. Armed with that map, the attacker chooses an evasion technique suited to the firewall in the way — and those techniques are the subject of the next section. The defensive lesson is the mirror image: the fewer things a firewall allows, and the more deeply it inspects them, the less room an attacker has to hide.

🥷 Evasion Techniques

Defeating recognition

This is the offensive heart of the module. Every technique here serves the single goal established in the overview: make malicious traffic unrecognizable to the defenses in its path — invisible to detection, acceptable to filtering, or both. The techniques fall into a few families based on how they defeat recognition: splitting the attack so no single piece looks bad, disguising its content so signatures don't match, hiding it inside trusted traffic, or exploiting the defense's own blind spots. Understanding the families matters more than memorizing every variant.

Fragmentation and splitting — the heart of IDS evasion

The foundational IDS/firewall evasion technique is packet fragmentation. A malicious payload is split across multiple small IP fragments; each fragment, examined alone, looks harmless, so a device that inspects packets in isolation sees nothing to match against — but the fragments reassemble at the target host and execute the attack. This works whenever the defense fails to reassemble the traffic the way the destination will. A closely related refinement is session splicing, which divides the attack's signature across multiple TCP segments so the malicious pattern never appears in any single packet the IDS inspects. And tiny fragments can defeat firewalls that only check the TCP header in the first fragment, since the crucial data is pushed into later fragments the firewall doesn't scrutinize. Fragmentation is called "the heart" of these techniques because it is simple, general, and defeats the most common inspection weakness — inspecting pieces rather than the reassembled whole.

Obfuscation and encoding — disguising the content

Where fragmentation splits the attack, obfuscation disguises it, so signature-based detection fails to match even when it sees the whole payload. Several methods achieve this:

🔤 Encoding

Representing a malicious string in an alternate form — Unicode, hex, URL-encoding, or Base64 — that the target decodes and executes but that doesn't match a signature written for the plain-text version. A classic way to sneak attack strings past pattern-matchers.

🧬 Polymorphic code

Attack code that changes its own appearance each time while keeping its function, so it never matches a fixed signature. The same idea that makes polymorphic malware (Module 7) hard to detect, applied to evasion — every instance looks different to a signature engine.

🎭 Protocol obfuscation

Manipulating standard protocols in unconventional-but-valid ways so malicious traffic blends with legitimate communication and slips past signature detection. Using unexpected encodings, casing, or structure that the target still accepts.

⏱️ Timing (slow and low)

Spreading attack traffic out over a long period so it stays under the rate-based thresholds an IDS uses to spot bursts. Patience defeats detectors tuned to notice fast, obvious activity — the same T0/T1 principle from the scanning module.

Tunneling and encryption — hiding inside trusted traffic

The most powerful firewall-evasion family hides forbidden traffic inside a protocol the firewall permits. Since firewalls almost always allow web traffic (and often DNS and ICMP), attackers wrap their traffic in these channels:

HTTP/HTTPS tunneling — hide C2 or exfil inside web traffic (almost always allowed) DNS tunneling — encode data in DNS queries/responses (often unmonitored) ICMP tunneling — hide data in ping packets (RFC 792 leaves the data field loose) SSH tunneling — encrypt and forward traffic through an SSH connection ACK tunneling — abuse ACK packets that stateless firewalls don't inspect

The special power of encryption here cannot be overstated: a defense that can't read a payload can't match a signature against it. Tunneling attack traffic through HTTPS or SSH both disguises it as normal traffic and hides its content from inspection — which is why so much modern malware uses encrypted C2 channels, and why deep TLS inspection has become a defensive necessity. Related firewall tricks include port hopping (moving across ports to evade port-based rules), proxy chaining (routing through multiple proxies to obscure the origin), IP/MAC spoofing (masquerading as a trusted source), and exploiting misconfigurations directly.

How the families fit together

A sophisticated intrusion rarely uses one technique; it layers them. An attacker might fragment the packets (defeat packet inspection), encode the payload (defeat signatures), tunnel the whole thing through HTTPS (defeat the firewall and hide the content), and spread it slowly over time (defeat rate thresholds) — each layer defeating a different defense. Recall too that Module 3 covered the scan-level versions of several of these (decoys, timing, fragmentation via Nmap's `-f`); this module extends them from evading a port scan to evading a full attack. The through-line is constant: every technique defeats recognition, and the defender's task, taken up in the countermeasures section, is to inspect deeply and completely enough that the disguise fails.

🍯 Honeypots

Turning the attacker's curiosity into a trap

A honeypot is a decoy system deliberately designed to look like an attractive target — a vulnerable server, an exposed database, a juicy file share — but with no legitimate production purpose. This gives it a remarkable property: any interaction with a honeypot is inherently suspicious, because no real user has any reason to touch it. That makes honeypots a very high-signal detection tool, almost free of the false positives that plague other systems. Beyond detection, honeypots let defenders study attackers — observing their tools, techniques, and targets — and waste their time, luring them into engaging with a fake system while the real ones stay safe. They flip the usual dynamic: instead of the attacker using reconnaissance against the target, the target uses the attacker's own probing against them.

Types by interaction level

Honeypots are classified chiefly by how much they let an attacker interact with them — the central trade-off being realism versus risk and effort.

🟢 Low-interaction

Emulates only a few basic services or protocols (a fake SSH or HTTP that responds but offers no real system behind it). Simple and safe to deploy, and low-risk since there's no real OS to compromise — but its limited, fixed behavior makes it easier for attackers to fingerprint and avoid.

🟡 Medium-interaction

Emulates vulnerable services more convincingly, producing valid replies for specific applications, without a full operating system. A middle ground — more believable than low-interaction, less complex and risky than high-interaction.

🔴 High-interaction

Provides a real operating system for the attacker to fully engage with. The most realistic and informative — ideal for observing complete attacker behavior, malware, and ransomware — but complex to maintain and the riskiest, since a real system could actually be compromised and turned against the network.

A useful family of related "honey-" deceptions extends the idea: honeynets (whole decoy networks), honeytokens (fake credentials or files that trigger an alert when used), and honeypot-as-a-service offerings. Tools range from classic emulators like Honeyd to modern platforms like T-Pot.

Detecting and evading honeypots

From the attacker's side, the goal is the reverse: identify honeypots and avoid them, because attacking a honeypot means being watched, wasting effort, and revealing your techniques to defenders. Attackers fingerprint suspected honeypots by looking for tells that distinguish a decoy from a real system:

⏱️ Behavioral tells

Abnormal response latency, the limited or fixed behavior of emulated services, responses that don't match what a real system would do, and services that seem too inviting or too shallow. Low-interaction honeypots especially give themselves away by not behaving like a full system.

🔍 System & deployment tells

Suspicious file-system characteristics (too few files, wrong directory depth), known honeypot signatures and artifacts, timing inconsistencies in file metadata, and evidence of standardized deployment scripts that many honeypots share. Internet-scale scans can fingerprint whole classes of off-the-shelf honeypots.

This is why honeypot fidelity matters so much: the more a honeypot behaves like a genuine system, the harder it is to fingerprint and avoid. It is the same cat-and-mouse as the rest of the module, played from the opposite side — the defender tries to make the trap indistinguishable from reality, and the attacker tries to spot the seams.

The modern, AI-driven arms race

Honeypots are evolving quickly in response to smarter, automated attacks. The 2026 landscape includes AI-adaptive honeypots that dynamically change their behavior to appear more realistic and resist fingerprinting, cloud-managed and honeypot-as-a-service deployments that lower the operational burden, and distributed honeynets that share attack data to spot campaigns early. Researchers are even using large language models to generate convincing honeytokens and to power intelligent-interaction honeypots that respond credibly to whatever an attacker tries. This mirrors the course-wide theme: as attacks become more automated and AI-driven, defenses — including deception — are becoming AI-driven too, and the fidelity contest between realistic decoys and fingerprinting attackers is intensifying on both sides.

🛡️ Countermeasures

Beating evasion means removing the blind spots

If every evasion technique works by defeating recognition, then countering evasion means closing the gaps that let disguised traffic go unrecognized. The defender's task is to inspect deeply enough, completely enough, and intelligently enough that the attacker's disguises fail — while accepting that no single system catches everything, so defenses must be layered. This section assembles the specific counters to the techniques from earlier in the module into a coherent defensive posture.

Inspect the whole, not the pieces

The direct answer to fragmentation and splicing — the heart of IDS evasion — is traffic reassembly. An IDS/IPS or NGFW must reassemble fragmented packets and reconstruct sessions before inspecting them, so it sees the same complete payload the target host will, rather than a stream of individually-innocent pieces. Modern systems do this, which is precisely why fragmentation is far less effective against a well-configured NGFW than against a simple packet filter. The general principle: inspect traffic in the form the target will actually receive it, not the form it happens to arrive in on the wire.

Layered, defense-in-depth detection

🔀 Combine detection methods

Since signature detection misses novel attacks and anomaly detection misses those that look normal, run both — plus stateful protocol analysis. What one method's blind spot lets through, another may catch. No single detection philosophy is sufficient alone.

🔓 Decrypt and inspect

Because encryption hides payloads from inspection, deploy TLS/SSL inspection at the perimeter so encrypted tunnels can be examined rather than trusted blindly. This directly counters the HTTPS-tunneling that hides so much modern malicious traffic.

🧱 Modernize the firewall

Next-generation firewalls with deep packet inspection and application awareness close the blind spots that stateless and stateful-only firewalls leave open — identifying tunneled and port-hopping traffic by what it actually is, not just its port.

📈 Normalize and monitor egress

Traffic normalization strips the ambiguities evasion relies on. Monitoring outbound traffic catches tunneling and C2 that inbound-focused defenses miss — unusual DNS volumes, unexpected ICMP payloads, connections to strange destinations.

Configuration, tuning, and detection-in-depth

Many real-world evasions succeed not against sophisticated systems but against misconfigured ones, so disciplined configuration is itself a countermeasure: least-privilege firewall rules that allow only what's needed, regular rule audits to remove permissive exceptions, and keeping signatures and detection engines current. Crucially, defenses must be tuned to catch evasion specifically — configured to reassemble fragments, flag improbable protocol behavior, correlate slow-and-low patterns over long windows, and alert on the anomalies that disguised traffic still produces. A system tuned only for obvious attacks gives false confidence against a skilled adversary, exactly as noted in the scanning module. And because a passive IDS only alerts, detection must be paired with timely response — a SIEM correlating signals and analysts (or automation) acting on them — since detection without response stops nothing.

Deception and the AI frontier

Honeypots and honeytokens deserve a place in the defensive stack precisely because they sidestep the recognition problem entirely: they don't try to distinguish good traffic from bad, they simply flag any interaction with something no legitimate user should touch. Well-deployed deception catches attackers who successfully evaded every other control. And looking forward, the decisive shift is toward AI/ML-driven detection: modern EDR/XDR platforms combine kernel-level behavioral monitoring with machine-learning anomaly analysis across telemetry, and the effect on evasion is dramatic — classic techniques that once slipped past defenses (standard credential-dumping, common injection methods, familiar tooling) now show detection rates above 85% in modern environments. As the course's AI theme predicts, artificial intelligence is compressing the survival space for evasion, pushing attackers toward ever more novel methods and defenders toward ever smarter detection.

The unifying lesson of the module: evasion and detection are a permanent, co-evolving contest, and the defender wins not with any single perfect system but with defense in depth — layered, deep, well-tuned inspection, plus deception and rapid response — so that traffic slipping past one control is caught by the next. Understanding both sides is what makes an ethical hacker able to test defenses honestly and strengthen them intelligently.

The through-line

Every evasion technique exploits the same mistake — inspecting the wrong thing: a fragment instead of the whole, a raw form instead of the decoded one, a port instead of the behaviour, a short interval instead of the long trend. The defences all correct the frame of reference: reassemble as the host will, normalize before matching, judge behaviour over appearance, and correlate across time. Signature detection catches the known fast; anomaly detection catches the unknown; together they turn an attempted bypass into a logged, detected event.
