🎯 Introduction to Ethical Hacking

What ethical hacking is — and what makes it ethical

Ethical hacking is the practice of deliberately probing systems, networks, and applications for security weaknesses using the same tools and techniques a malicious attacker would — but with authorization, and for the purpose of fixing what you find rather than exploiting it. The skills are identical to those of a criminal hacker; what separates the two is not capability but permission, intent, and disclosure. An ethical hacker operates under an explicit agreement, aims to strengthen the target's defenses, and reports every weakness discovered so it can be remediated. Remove any one of those and the same actions become a crime.

This is the foundational module of the course, and its job is to establish the mental framework that everything afterward builds on. Before you learn how to enumerate a network or crack a password, you need to understand why you're doing it, how attackers think, how their attacks are structured, and — critically — the legal and ethical boundaries that make the difference between a penetration tester and a felon. The technical modules that follow are the "how"; this module is the "why" and the "within what limits."

Think like an attacker to defend like a professional

The central premise of ethical hacking is deceptively simple: you cannot defend against an attack you don't understand. A defender who has never seen how a phishing email leads to credential theft, how a misconfigured share leaks a user list, or how a single unpatched service becomes a foothold, is defending against an imagined threat rather than a real one. By learning to think and act like an attacker — mapping targets, finding weaknesses, chaining them into a compromise — the ethical hacker gains the perspective needed to anticipate and close those paths before a real adversary walks them. This "offensive mindset in service of defense" is the thread that runs through every module of the course.

What this module covers

The module lays out the conceptual toolkit of the discipline. The CIA Triad defines what security actually means — the three properties every control ultimately protects. Hacker types map the spectrum of actors, from criminals to the ethical professionals you're training to become. The Cyber Kill Chain and MITRE ATT&CK are the two frameworks that structure how we understand attacks — one high-level and linear, the other granular and behavioral. AI-driven security examines how artificial intelligence is reshaping both attack and defense. And the legal and ethical section establishes the boundaries — the authorization, scope, and disclosure rules — that govern everything. Master these, and the technical modules become applications of a framework you already understand rather than a disconnected collection of techniques.

🔐 The CIA Triad

The definition of security itself

Before you can protect a system, you need a precise answer to the question "protect it from what?" The CIA Triad — Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability — is that answer, and it is the single most fundamental model in all of information security. Every security control that has ever been built, and every attack that has ever been launched, ultimately concerns one or more of these three properties. When you understand an attack in terms of which of the three it violates, you understand what it actually threatens and what defending against it requires. The triad is not academic trivia; it is the lens through which the entire field is organized.

The three pillars

Each pillar protects a distinct property, and each is attacked in characteristic ways — a framing worth carrying into every later module, because you'll find each attack maps cleanly onto one or more of these.

🔒 Confidentiality

Ensuring information is accessible only to those authorized to see it. Violated by data breaches, eavesdropping, and theft — the sniffing and enumeration attacks later in the course are largely confidentiality attacks. Protected by encryption, access controls, and authentication.

✏️ Integrity

Ensuring information is accurate and unaltered, and can only be changed by authorized parties. Violated by tampering, unauthorized modification, and corruption — a defaced website or an altered financial record. Protected by hashing, digital signatures, and change controls.

⏱️ Availability

Ensuring systems and data are accessible to authorized users when needed. Violated by denial-of-service attacks and ransomware — the entire DoS module is an assault on availability. Protected by redundancy, backups, and resilient infrastructure.

A useful discipline is to classify every attack you learn by which pillar it targets. A password breach attacks confidentiality; ransomware attacks availability (and, via data theft, confidentiality); tampering with logs to cover tracks attacks integrity. This classification is not just tidy — it tells you immediately what kind of defense applies.

🔐 Confidentiality

Protecting information from unauthorized access

✅ Integrity

Ensuring data accuracy and trustworthiness

⚡ Availability

Guaranteeing access to authorized users

The balancing act — and its extensions

The three pillars are in constant tension, and security is largely the art of balancing them for a given context. Maximum confidentiality — encrypting everything, requiring many authentication factors — can impair availability by making access slow or fragile. Maximum availability can undermine confidentiality. The right balance depends entirely on what's being protected: a public website prioritizes availability, a classified database prioritizes confidentiality, a financial ledger prioritizes integrity. Beyond the core three, practitioners often add authentication (verifying identity), authorization (granting appropriate access), and non-repudiation (ensuring an action can't later be denied) — extensions that refine the model for real systems. But the core triad remains the foundation, and "which pillar does this affect?" is a question worth asking of every control and every attack in this course.

👥 Hacker Types

The spectrum of intent

"Hacker" is a broad term that spans a wide range of actors, and the security industry classifies them primarily by intent and authorization — the same two qualities that define ethical hacking itself. The classic shorthand borrows from old Western films, where heroes wore white hats and villains wore black. Understanding this spectrum matters because it clarifies exactly where the ethical hacker sits, what distinguishes them legally and morally from the criminals they emulate, and why the same technical skill can be either a profession or a felony depending on who wields it and why.

The three hats — and the space between

⚪ White Hat

The ethical hacker. Operates with explicit authorization, aims to improve security, and discloses findings responsibly. Penetration testers, security researchers, and the professionals this course trains. Their defining trait is permission and constructive intent.

⚫ Black Hat

The malicious attacker. Acts without authorization for personal gain, disruption, or harm — data theft, extortion, sabotage. Uses the same techniques as the white hat, but illegally and destructively. The adversary the ethical hacker learns to think like in order to defeat.

⚪⚫ Gray Hat

The ambiguous middle. May probe systems without authorization but without malicious intent — for example, finding a flaw uninvited and then reporting it. Well-intentioned perhaps, but still crossing the legal line, which is why "I meant well" is no defense: the missing ingredient is authorization.

The gray hat is the most instructive category precisely because it isolates the single variable that matters most: authorization. A gray hat may have good intentions and may even do a service by reporting a bug — but without permission to test in the first place, they have still broken the law. This is the sharpest possible illustration of the module's core lesson, and it is why the legal section treats authorization as non-negotiable.

Beyond the hats: motivation-based categories

The color scheme captures authorization, but real-world threat actors are also classified by motivation and capability, which matters for understanding who an organization is defending against. Script kiddies are unskilled attackers who run tools others built, dangerous mainly through volume and opportunism. Hacktivists attack to advance a political or social cause. Cybercriminals and organized crime groups are financially motivated and increasingly professional — the ransomware and infostealer economies from the malware module. Nation-state actors and Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs) are the most capable: well-resourced, patient, and stealthy, pursuing espionage or strategic disruption over months or years. And insider threats — malicious or negligent employees — sit inside the perimeter entirely, bypassing many defenses by default. Knowing which of these an organization realistically faces shapes what it should prioritize defending against.

⛓️ The Cyber Kill Chain

Attacks have structure — and structure means opportunity

Attacks are not single events; they are processes that unfold in stages, and the Cyber Kill Chain — developed by Lockheed Martin — is the model that captures this. It breaks a cyberattack into a linear sequence of seven stages, from an attacker's first reconnaissance to their final objective. The military term "kill chain" refers to the structure of an attack; the defensive insight it delivers is powerful: because an attack must pass through each stage in order, disrupting the chain at any single point stops the entire attack. A defender does not have to catch every stage — breaking one link is enough. This reframes defense from an impossible "block everything" into a tractable "break the chain somewhere," and it is why the model has endured.

1. Reconnaissance

Attackers gather information about the target through passive and active techniques

2. Weaponization

Creating attack vectors like malware, exploits, or payload delivery mechanisms

3. Delivery

Transmitting the weapon to the target via email, websites, or removable media

4. Exploitation

Triggering the vulnerability and executing malicious code on the target system

5. Installation

Installing persistent access mechanisms like backdoors or rootkits

6. Command & Control (C2)

Establishing communication channels for remote system control

7. Actions on Objectives

Achieving the ultimate goal: data exfiltration, system destruction, or espionage

The seven stages

Each stage represents a broad category of attacker activity, and — usefully for a course — each maps to modules you'll study in depth later.

Reconnaissance gathers information about the target (the footprinting and scanning phases). Weaponization couples an exploit with a payload — often malware — into a deliverable package. Delivery transmits it, most commonly via phishing (the social engineering module). Exploitation triggers the vulnerability to execute code on the victim. Installation plants persistent access — backdoors and RATs from the malware module. Command and Control opens the channel through which the attacker remotely directs the compromised system. Finally, Actions on Objectives is where the attacker achieves their actual goal — stealing data, deploying ransomware, or moving deeper into the network.

Defending across the chain

The model's practical value is that it tells defenders where they can intervene, and that different defenses target different stages. Blocking reconnaissance is hard, but you can defend delivery with email filtering, exploitation with patching, installation with endpoint detection, and command-and-control by monitoring outbound network traffic for anomalous connections. Because breaking any one link defeats the whole attack, a layered defense that places controls at multiple stages is remarkably resilient — an attacker who slips past one control still faces the next. This is the strategic logic behind defense-in-depth, and the kill chain is the map that shows where each layer belongs.

🎯 MITRE ATT&CK Framework

From "what stages" to "exactly how"

Where the Cyber Kill Chain describes attacks at a high level — broad stages in a fixed order — the MITRE ATT&CK framework goes deeper, cataloging the specific behaviors attackers actually use. ATT&CK stands for Adversarial Tactics, Techniques, and Common Knowledge, and MITRE has maintained it since 2013 (publicly released in 2015) as a continuously-updated knowledge base of real-world adversary behavior, drawn from observed attacks. It has become the industry-standard vocabulary for describing how attacks work — the common language that lets a threat report, a detection rule, and a red-team exercise all refer to the same specific technique unambiguously.

Tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs)

ATT&CK is organized as a hierarchy, and understanding its three levels is the key to using it. Tactics are the attacker's high-level goals — the "why" of an action, such as gaining initial access, escalating privileges, or exfiltrating data. Enterprise ATT&CK currently defines 14 tactics. Techniques are the ways those goals are achieved — the "how" — and there are currently around 188 techniques with roughly 379 sub-techniques beneath them, each with a unique identifier (for example, T1566 for Phishing). Procedures are the specific real-world implementations a particular threat actor uses. Together these are called TTPs, and they are how modern security teams describe adversary behavior with precision. Saying an attacker "used T1566.001 for initial access and T1003.001 to dump credentials" communicates exactly what happened in a way everyone in the field understands.

Kill Chain vs. ATT&CK: complementary, not competing

A common point of confusion — and a favorite exam question — is how these two frameworks relate. They are not competitors; they operate at different levels and work best together. The Cyber Kill Chain is high-level, linear, and intentionally simplified: it tells you the broad stages of an attack in order, which is ideal for understanding an attack's overall shape and for strategic planning. MITRE ATT&CK is granular, behavioral, and non-linear: its tactics are unordered and may not all appear in a single intrusion, because it catalogs specific behaviors rather than a fixed sequence.

The two combine naturally. Use the Kill Chain to understand where in an attack's lifecycle you are, then use ATT&CK to identify the exact technique in play and how to detect or mitigate it. A security team investigating a breach might use the Kill Chain to see that an attack progressed from reconnaissance through exploitation, then use the ATT&CK matrix to pinpoint that the attacker used spearphishing (T1566.001) for delivery and LSASS memory dumping (T1003.001) for credential access — and tune their detection rules accordingly. Strategic visibility from the Kill Chain, tactical depth from ATT&CK.

Why this matters for ethical hackers

For the offensive professional this course trains, ATT&CK is doubly valuable. As a red teamer, you use it to emulate real adversaries — structuring an engagement around the actual techniques a relevant threat actor uses, so your test reflects genuine risk rather than arbitrary probing. As someone who ultimately serves the defense, you use ATT&CK's mapping of techniques to detections and mitigations to help organizations find and close the gaps in their coverage. This is the essence of the purple team mindset — offense and defense working from the same framework — and ATT&CK is the shared language that makes it possible. Nearly every subsequent module in this course maps to specific ATT&CK techniques, which is why establishing the framework here matters.

🤖 AI-Driven Security

The defining shift of the current era

Artificial intelligence has become the most consequential force reshaping cybersecurity, transforming both how attacks are launched and how they are defended against. For the ethical hacker entering the field now, understanding this shift is not optional — it is the context in which every technique in this course now operates. The essential framing to carry through the whole course is that AI is a dual-use amplifier: the same capabilities that let defenders detect threats faster let attackers launch them faster, and the contest is increasingly between AI-enhanced offense and AI-enhanced defense.

AI on both sides

⚔️ AI empowering attackers

Attackers use AI to write and mutate malware (the polymorphic and AI-generated threats from the malware module), craft convincing phishing at scale, generate deepfakes for impersonation, discover vulnerabilities faster, and adapt their behavior in real time to evade detection. AI lowers the skill barrier and raises the speed and sophistication of attacks across the board.

🛡️ AI empowering defenders

Defenders use AI to detect anomalies no hand-written rule could anticipate, correlate signals across endpoints, identity, and network to catch attacks that look benign in isolation, prioritize vulnerabilities by predicted exploitation (the EPSS model from the vulnerability module), and automate response at machine speed. As attacks accelerate, AI-driven detection becomes a necessity rather than a luxury.

New attack surfaces — and the ethical hacker's expanding role

AI does more than accelerate existing attacks; it creates entirely new things to attack. As organizations embed AI systems and large language models into their workflows — often with real permissions and access to sensitive data — those systems become targets in their own right. Prompt injection, data poisoning, and model manipulation are emerging vulnerability classes that did not exist a few years ago. This expands the ethical hacker's remit: increasingly, securing an organization means testing its AI systems too, probing them for the new weaknesses that come with this new technology. The offensive professional's job now includes understanding how AI systems fail and can be subverted.

Fundamentals still win

It is worth ending with the balanced perspective this course returns to whenever AI comes up. AI genuinely raises the stakes — attacks are faster, more evasive, and more accessible than ever. But it does not repeal the fundamentals. The CIA triad still defines what you're protecting; the kill chain and ATT&CK still describe how attacks unfold; authorization and disclosure still define ethical practice; and least privilege, patching, segmentation, and monitoring still form the backbone of defense. AI is a powerful amplifier layered on top of these foundations, not a replacement for them. The ethical hacker who masters the fundamentals in this course and then learns to apply and defend against AI is exactly the professional the field now needs.

⚖️ Legal & Ethical Framework

The single most important module in the course

Everything else in this course teaches capability. This section teaches the boundary that determines whether that capability makes you a valued professional or a criminal defendant. It is not an afterthought or a disclaimer — it is the foundation on which ethical hacking as a legitimate profession stands. The techniques you will learn are, applied without authorization, serious crimes in nearly every jurisdiction on earth. What transforms them into a respected career is a framework of law, authorization, and ethics that you must understand as thoroughly as any technical skill. An expert hacker who ignores this framework is not an ethical hacker; they are simply a criminal with good skills.

Authorization: the line that defines everything

If there is one concept to carry out of this entire module, it is this: authorization is what separates ethical hacking from crime, and it must be explicit, written, and specific. A verbal "sure, go ahead" is not authorization. A vague "test our security" is not sufficient scope. Proper authorization takes the form of a signed agreement that defines exactly what you are permitted to test (the scope), when, by what methods, and what to do if you find something. This document — often a statement of work paired with a rules-of-engagement agreement — is not bureaucratic overhead; it is the legal instrument that makes your work lawful, and the boundary that protects both you and the client.

The gray-hat problem from the hacker-types section returns here with full force: good intentions do not substitute for authorization. Discovering a vulnerability in a system you were not authorized to test is still illegal access, no matter how helpfully you report it. The rule is absolute — test only what you have explicit, written permission to test, and nothing beyond that scope.

The laws that govern the work

Ethical hackers operate within a web of computer-crime laws, and while the specifics vary by country, the principles are consistent. In the United States, the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) is the primary statute criminalizing unauthorized access to computer systems; similar laws exist worldwide, such as the UK's Computer Misuse Act. Beyond access laws, ethical hackers must respect data protection regulations — the EU's GDPR, healthcare's HIPAA, payment-card PCI DSS — which govern how any personal or sensitive data encountered during testing must be handled. A penetration test that accesses a system lawfully can still create liability if it mishandles the personal data it finds. Knowing which laws and regulations apply to a given engagement is part of scoping it properly.

Ignorance is not a defense. "I didn't know it was illegal" and "I was only trying to help" carry no legal weight. Before any engagement, an ethical hacker confirms they have proper written authorization, understands the applicable laws, and stays strictly within the agreed scope. When in doubt, the professional answer is always to stop and confirm authorization rather than proceed.

Responsible disclosure and professional ethics

Finding a vulnerability creates a responsibility, and how you handle that responsibility is a defining mark of the ethical professional. Responsible disclosure means reporting a vulnerability privately to the affected organization, giving them reasonable time to fix it before any public discussion — never exploiting it for gain and never publicizing it in a way that helps attackers before a fix exists. Many organizations now run formal bug bounty programs and publish vulnerability disclosure policies that provide a legal, structured channel for exactly this. Beyond disclosure, professional ethics demand confidentiality (protecting whatever sensitive information you encounter), integrity (reporting findings honestly and completely), and staying within scope. Professional certifications like the CEH include a code of ethics that codifies these duties. The through-line of the entire module lands here: the ethical hacker's power comes with the obligation to use it only with permission, only within bounds, and only in service of making systems safer.
