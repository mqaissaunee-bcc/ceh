CEH v13 Module 3: Scanning Networks

Scanning Networks

Turning reconnaissance into a live map: host discovery, TCP/UDP port scanning and scan types, OS and service fingerprinting, firewall/IDS evasion, and the Nmap-centred tooling that drives it.

⏱️ 3+ hours 🧪 Hands-on labs 📝 Assessment 🔬 Interactive demos

🚪 Overview

💡Think of it like this

Scanning is walking the building at night and trying every door handle.

Footprinting gave you the address; scanning tells you which doors exist, which are locked, and what kind of lock is fitted. Each port is a door and an open port is one that turns. It is noisy work, and it converts a guessed map into a verified one.

Where the analogy breaks down: Door handles don't remember being tried. Every packet you send is a log entry, and a full scan is among the loudest things you can do on a network.

From map to live territory

Reconnaissance (Module 2) produced a map of the target — its domains, IP ranges, and likely infrastructure — mostly from public sources. Scanning is the phase where the tester interacts with that map directly to see what is actually there and alive. Where footprinting asked "what does the target look like from the outside?", scanning asks the sharper questions: "which hosts are up, which ports are open, what services are running, and what versions?" It is the bridge between passive intelligence and active engagement — the point where you begin sending packets to the target and interpreting what comes back.

This makes scanning both powerful and consequential. It reveals the concrete attack surface — the specific doors and windows of the target — that every later phase depends on. It is also unambiguously active: scanning touches the target's systems, appears in their logs, and can trip their defenses. For that reason it always requires authorization, and a careless scan can even disrupt fragile systems. As one CEH study guide stresses, obtaining permission before interacting with target systems is the first rule, because scans carry real risk.

The three kinds of scanning

The CEH methodology divides scanning into three complementary activities, each answering a different question:

🌐 Network scanning

Discovering which hosts are live on a network — the "host discovery" or "ping sweep" step. Before probing ports, you need to know which addresses actually respond, so you don't waste effort scanning empty space.

🚪 Port scanning

Discovering which ports are open on a live host, and therefore which services it exposes. The heart of the phase — an open port is a potential entry point, and the pattern of open ports characterizes the host.

🐞 Vulnerability scanning

Checking the discovered services against known weaknesses. This shades into Module 5 (Vulnerability Analysis), but scanners like Nmap's scripting engine can flag known vulnerabilities as part of the scan itself.

Why scanning rewards patience

It is tempting to fire a single aggressive scan and move on, but skilled testers treat scanning as an art worth mastering. The principle, as one practitioner puts it, is that information is power: without launching a single exploit, careful scanning reveals entry points, misconfigurations, and patterns that shape the entire engagement. The type of scan chosen, its timing, and its stealth all matter — an aggressive scan is fast but loud and may be blocked or may crash a delicate service, while a patient, well-chosen scan gathers accurate information while staying under detection thresholds. Understanding the trade-offs between scan types is what separates a novice running default commands from a professional who knows exactly what each probe reveals and costs.

What this module covers

We start with the methodology — the disciplined scanning process and where it fits after reconnaissance. Then the technical core: port scanning (the TCP three-way handshake and the family of scan types built on it), and discovery and fingerprinting (finding live hosts, then identifying their operating systems and service versions). We cover evasion — how scans are crafted to slip past firewalls and intrusion-detection systems — before surveying the tools, centered on Nmap, and closing with the countermeasures defenders use to detect and blunt scanning. The output of this module is a detailed inventory of live hosts, open ports, and identified services — precisely what the enumeration phase in Module 4 needs to dig deeper.

Scanning is active and requires authorization. Unlike passive reconnaissance, every scan sends packets to systems you do not own. Doing so without explicit written permission can violate computer-crime laws and, in rare cases, disrupt the target. Scan only assets that are within an authorized engagement scope, and practice these techniques only in sanctioned lab environments.

🧭 Methodology

💡Think of it like this

A scanning methodology is a triage nurse's order of operations.

Check breathing before setting the arm. Host discovery first — is anything alive? Then port scanning: what is open? Then service and version detection: what is it actually? Only then vulnerability inference. Skip a step and you spend an afternoon enumerating a host that was decommissioned last year.

Where the analogy breaks down: A nurse handles one patient. Scanning thousands of hosts makes ordering a pipeline problem, and the aggressive parallelism that makes it fast is exactly what trips detection.

A disciplined sequence

Scanning done well follows a logical order, each step narrowing the focus of the next. Firing every kind of scan at every address is wasteful and loud; a methodical approach is faster, quieter, and more accurate. The sequence flows naturally from "what's alive?" to "what's open?" to "what is it, exactly?"

First, host discovery identifies which addresses in the target range actually respond, so subsequent effort is spent only on live hosts. Next, port scanning probes those live hosts to find open ports — the services they expose. Then service and version detection identifies precisely what software is behind each open port, and OS fingerprinting determines the operating system. Each step enriches the profile, and the final result is a detailed inventory: for every live host, its operating system and a list of open ports with the exact services and versions running on them.

Understanding ports and services

To make sense of port scanning, you need a clear mental model of what a port is. A network host runs many services simultaneously — a web server, a mail server, a database — and ports are the numbered channels (0–65535) that let a single IP address distinguish between them. Certain "well-known" ports conventionally host certain services: 80 and 443 for web (HTTP/HTTPS), 22 for SSH, 25 for mail (SMTP), 53 for DNS, 3389 for Windows Remote Desktop. When a scanner reports that port 22 is open, it is inferring that the host is probably running SSH and is willing to accept connections there. This is why open ports matter so much: each one represents a running service that can be interrogated, and potentially exploited.

A scanner classifies each probed port into a state. Open means a service is actively accepting connections. Closed means the port is reachable but no service is listening. Filtered means a firewall or filter is blocking the probe, so the scanner cannot tell whether the port is open — an important distinction, because "filtered" often signals a firewall's presence.

The TCP three-way handshake: the foundation

Nearly every scan technique is a variation on how a TCP connection is normally established, so this handshake is the bedrock concept of the whole module. To open a TCP connection, three packets are exchanged in order:

Client ── SYN ──▶ Server (1) "I'd like to connect" Client ◀── SYN-ACK ── Server (2) "OK, I'm listening" Client ── ACK ──▶ Server (3) "Great, connection established"

This SYN → SYN-ACK → ACK exchange is how legitimate connections begin. The insight that powers port scanning is that the server's response to the first packet already reveals the port's state: a server whose port is open answers a SYN with SYN-ACK, while a closed port answers with RST (reset). A scanner does not need to complete the handshake to learn what it wants — and, as the next section shows, the various scan types differ precisely in how much of the handshake they complete and which flags they manipulate, trading detectability against reliability.

🚪 Port Scanning

💡Think of it like this

A TCP handshake is a knock, and scan types differ in how politely you knock.

A full connect scan knocks, waits, shakes hands and introduces itself — reliable and thoroughly logged. A SYN scan knocks and walks away before the door fully opens. FIN, NULL and Xmas scans send deliberately nonsensical knocks to see how the door reacts, since different stacks respond differently to nonsense.

Where the analogy breaks down: Politeness only fools a doorman following the rulebook. Stateful firewalls and modern IDS recognize half-open patterns immediately, so “stealth” scans are mostly stealthy against 1998.

One goal, many techniques

Every port-scanning technique aims to answer the same question — is this port open? — but they differ in how they ask, and those differences matter enormously for speed, stealth, reliability, and the ability to slip past firewalls. The CEH exam tests these scan types heavily, and understanding the mechanics (not just the flags) is what lets a tester choose the right one for a given situation. They are best understood as a family built on the TCP handshake from the previous section.

The two foundational TCP scans

🥷 SYN scan (-sS) — the default

Sends a SYN; if it gets SYN-ACK (open) it replies with RST to tear down the half-built connection rather than completing it. Because the full connection never opens, it was historically less likely to be logged — hence "half-open" or "stealth" scan. Fast and efficient, it is Nmap's default and most-used scan. Requires raw-socket (root) privileges.

🤝 TCP Connect scan (-sT)

Completes the full handshake (SYN → SYN-ACK → ACK). More detectable and more likely to be logged by the target application, and slower — but it needs no special privileges, because it uses the operating system's normal connect call. The fallback when you can't run a SYN scan.

The SYN-vs-Connect distinction is a favorite exam point. Both find open ports; the difference is that the SYN scan stops after the second packet (quieter, needs root) while the Connect scan finishes all three (louder, no root needed).

The stealth scans: FIN, NULL, and Xmas

A cleverer family exploits a subtle rule in the TCP specification (RFC 793) to probe ports while evading rules that only watch for SYN packets. These scans send packets with unusual flag combinations:

nmap -sF target # FIN scan — only the FIN flag set nmap -sN target # NULL scan — no flags set at all nmap -sX target # Xmas scan — FIN, PSH, and URG flags set ("lit up like a tree")

The trick: per RFC 793, when a packet arrives without SYN, RST, or ACK set, a closed port must reply with RST, while an open port sends no response at all. So the scanner infers "open (or filtered)" from silence and "closed" from an RST. Because these packets lack the SYN flag, they fly straight through firewall rules that only block SYN packets, and they are stealthier than even a SYN scan. The important caveat, heavily tested: this behavior relies on RFC compliance, and major systems including Windows do not follow it (they send RST for any probe), so these scans don't work reliably against Windows — they are mainly effective against Unix-like systems.

ACK scan and UDP scan

🧱 ACK scan (-sA)

Not for finding open ports, but for mapping firewalls. It sends a bare ACK; the response (or lack of one) reveals whether a port is filtered or unfiltered — that is, whether a stateful firewall sits in front of it. A tester uses it to understand the target's filtering rules rather than its services.

📡 UDP scan (-sU)

Scans UDP services (DNS on 53, SNMP on 161, DHCP), which the TCP scans miss entirely. UDP is connectionless, so scanning is slower and less certain — an open UDP port often simply stays silent — but it is essential, since services like DNS and SNMP (Module 4's enumeration targets) run over UDP.

Other specialized scans exist — the Idle scan (-sI), which bounces probes off a third "zombie" host to hide the scanner's identity entirely, and the Maimon scan, another RFC-exploiting variant — but the ones above cover the essential toolkit and the vast majority of exam questions.

Choosing and combining scans

The practical skill is matching the scan to the situation. Reach for the SYN scan (-sS) by default — fast, stealthy, reliable. Fall back to the Connect scan (-sT) when you lack root privileges. Use FIN/NULL/Xmas to attempt firewall evasion against Unix-like targets. Add the UDP scan (-sU) whenever UDP services matter, and the ACK scan (-sA) to map filtering. And critically, almost always combine the port scan with version detection (-sV) — covered next — because knowing that a port is open is far less useful than knowing exactly what software and version is behind it. A tester rarely runs a scan type in isolation; the real craft is composing the right combination for the target and the engagement's stealth requirements.

🔎 Discovery & Fingerprinting

💡Think of it like this

Fingerprinting is guessing someone's nationality from their handwriting.

Every operating system writes TCP packets with small idiosyncrasies — default TTL, window size, the order it lists options. No single one is secret, but together they are distinctive enough for nmap to say “Windows Server 2019” from a handful of packets. Banner grabbing is the blunt version: reading the sign on the door.

Where the analogy breaks down: Handwriting can be imitated. Banners are trivially edited and stacks can be tuned, so fingerprint output is a strong hypothesis and never proof.

Finding what's alive, then identifying it

This section covers the bookends of the scanning process: host discovery (which comes before port scanning, to find live targets) and fingerprinting (which comes after, to identify what those targets actually are). Together they transform a raw list of addresses into a rich profile — for each live host, its operating system and the exact software running on each open port. That profile is the real deliverable of the scanning phase.

Host discovery: who's home?

Before scanning ports, a tester finds which hosts in the target range are actually up — the "ping sweep" or host-discovery step — so effort isn't wasted probing dead addresses. Nmap does this automatically, and understanding its default behavior is worth knowing precisely (it's a common exam detail):

nmap -sn 10.10.1.0/24 # host discovery only, no port scan (ping sweep) nmap -Pn 10.10.1.5 # skip discovery, treat host as up (for hosts that block pings)

On a local network, Nmap uses ARP requests, which are fast and reliable because hosts must answer ARP to function. On a remote network, it sends a combination of probes — an ICMP echo request, a TCP SYN to port 443, a TCP ACK to port 80, and an ICMP timestamp request — casting a wide enough net that most hosts respond to at least one, even if some block plain pings. The -Pn option is the important counterpart: it skips discovery entirely and assumes the host is up, which is how you scan a host that silently drops ping probes (as many firewalled systems do) but is nonetheless online.

Service and version detection

Knowing that a port is open is only half the story; knowing exactly what is running on it is what makes the finding actionable. Nmap's version detection (-sV) probes each open port and matches the responses against a database to identify the specific software and version:

nmap -sV 10.10.1.5 # detect service and version on each open port # Example result: 80/tcp open http Apache httpd 2.4.58

The difference between "port 80 is open" and "port 80 is running Apache httpd 2.4.58" is the difference between a vague hint and a precise lead. With the exact version in hand, a tester can look up known vulnerabilities for that specific release — the bridge from scanning (this module) to vulnerability analysis (Module 5) and exploitation. This is also the mechanism behind banner grabbing, where the service's own identifying banner is captured; version detection is essentially a systematic, automated form of it.

OS fingerprinting

Identifying the target's operating system is the other half of fingerprinting, and it works by a clever observation: different operating systems implement the TCP/IP stack with subtle, characteristic differences — in how they set default values, respond to unusual packets, and handle edge cases. By sending a series of crafted probes and analyzing the exact pattern of responses, Nmap compares the target's "TCP/IP stack behavior" against a fingerprint database to guess the OS:

nmap -O 10.10.1.5 # OS detection via TCP/IP stack fingerprinting nmap -A 10.10.1.5 # aggressive: OS + version + scripts + traceroute together

This is active OS fingerprinting — it sends probes and is therefore detectable — as opposed to passive fingerprinting, which infers the OS from traffic already being observed. OS detection works best when Nmap can find at least one open and one closed port to compare, and the -A ("aggressive") flag bundles OS detection, version detection, script scanning, and traceroute into a single comprehensive scan. Knowing the OS narrows the field of applicable exploits and shapes the approach to every later phase — Windows and Linux targets are attacked very differently.

The Nmap Scripting Engine

Fingerprinting blurs into vulnerability detection through the Nmap Scripting Engine (NSE), a library of 600+ scripts that extend Nmap far beyond basic scanning. NSE scripts can grab detailed service information, enumerate shares and users (previewing Module 4), brute-force logins, and — most notably — check discovered services against known vulnerabilities:

nmap --script vuln 10.10.1.5 # run vulnerability-detection scripts nmap --script http-enum 10.10.1.5 # enumerate web server paths

With NSE, a single tool spans host discovery, port scanning, version and OS detection, and preliminary vulnerability checks — which is exactly why Nmap sits at the center of the toolkit in the next section. The scanning phase now hands the enumeration phase (Module 4) everything it needs: live hosts, open ports, identified services and versions, operating systems, and a first look at their weaknesses.

🥷 Evasion

💡Think of it like this

Scan evasion is arriving at the party in pieces so the bouncer never sees a whole guest.

Fragmentation splits a signature across packets the sensor must reassemble. Decoys surround your real address with twenty convincing fakes. Timing templates slow you beneath the alarm threshold. Source-port spoofing dresses your traffic as DNS, which the firewall waves through without looking.

Where the analogy breaks down: The bouncer improved. Reassembly is now standard, and slowing a scan below the threshold can mean weeks — so it is usually cheaper for an attacker to phish a password than to evade the sensor.

Scanning past the defenses

Real targets are protected by firewalls and intrusion-detection systems (IDS) that filter traffic and watch for the tell-tale patterns of a scan — bursts of connection attempts, unusual flag combinations, probes marching sequentially through ports. A naive scan against a defended target will be blocked, logged, and possibly alerted on. Evasion techniques are the methods testers use to make scans stealthier and slip past these defenses, and they matter for two reasons: an authorized red-team engagement often needs to test whether the defenses actually catch a scan, and understanding evasion is what lets defenders recognize and counter it. Every technique here is a lever trading stealth against speed or reliability.

The core evasion techniques

🧩 Packet fragmentation (-f)

Splits scan probes into tiny fragments so that a firewall or IDS inspecting individual packets can't recognize the probe's signature. The target's stack reassembles the fragments, but a filter that doesn't reassemble may let them through.

🎭 Decoys (-D)

Makes the scan appear to come from many source addresses at once, only one of which is real. The target's logs fill with probes from numerous IPs, burying the actual scanner's address among decoys so defenders can't easily tell who is really scanning.

🕰️ Timing control (-T0…-T5)

Slows the scan to stay under detection thresholds. Nmap's timing templates run from -T0 (paranoid) and -T1 (sneaky) — deliberately glacial to avoid triggering rate-based alerts — through -T3 (normal, the default) up to -T4/-T5 (aggressive/insane), which are fast but loud.

🎯 Spoofing & padding

Source-port spoofing (making traffic appear to come from a trusted port like 53 or 80), IP spoofing, and appending random data with --data-length to change packet sizes and defeat signature matching. Each disguises the scan's fingerprint.

Putting evasion together

In practice these techniques are combined into a single stealthy scan. A representative evasive Nmap invocation might fragment packets, skip host discovery, use decoys, and pad the packets all at once:

nmap -f -T1 -Pn -D 10.0.0.5,10.0.0.6,ME --data-length 200 target

Here -f fragments, -T1 throttles the timing, -Pn skips the ping stage (itself a way to avoid discovery probes that firewalls flag), -D adds decoys with the real scanner's position marked ME, and --data-length pads each packet. The stealth gained comes at a cost — such a scan is far slower and can be less reliable — which is the constant trade-off of evasion. Note too that the stealth "FIN/NULL/Xmas" scans from the port-scanning section are themselves evasion techniques, since they bypass firewall rules that only watch for SYN packets.

Why defenders must understand this

Evasion is not only an attacker's craft; it defines what a defender must be able to detect. A security team that only recognizes a fast, obvious default scan will miss a patient, fragmented, decoy-laden one — which is exactly what a real adversary would use. Studying evasion tells defenders what to tune their IDS/IPS for: fragmented packets that need reassembly before inspection, improbable flag combinations, traffic from many sources probing the same ports, and slow-and-low patterns spread over hours. This mutual understanding — attacker techniques informing defensive detection — is the purple-team mindset from Module 1, and it leads directly into the countermeasures that close this module.

🛠️ Tools

💡Think of it like this

If scanners were cameras, nmap is the SLR and masscan is the security camera.

masscan sweeps enormous ranges fast and shallow: this port is open, nothing more. nmap is slower and deliberate, with version detection, OS inference and a scripting engine. The professional workflow uses both — masscan to find the interesting few, nmap to understand them.

Where the analogy breaks down: A camera records what was there. Scanners infer, and both will confidently report a service that is really a honeypot or a load balancer answering on something else's behalf.

Nmap: the scanner that defines the field

If one tool dominates network scanning, it is Nmap (Network Mapper). It is free, open-source, endlessly capable, and so central that most of this module's commands have been Nmap commands. Nmap performs every stage of the scanning process — host discovery, port scanning in all the varieties covered earlier, service and version detection, OS fingerprinting, and (through its scripting engine) vulnerability checks — in a single tool. It is used in essentially every professional engagement and every CEH practical exam. Mastering Nmap is not optional; it is the core competency of this module.

A consolidated Nmap reference

Pulling together the options introduced across the module, these are the ones worth committing to memory:

# Scan types -sS SYN (stealth, default) -sT TCP connect (no root needed) -sU UDP scan -sA ACK scan (firewall mapping) -sF -sN -sX FIN / NULL / Xmas (stealth, RFC-based) # Discovery and identification -sn host discovery only -Pn skip discovery (assume up) -sV service/version detection -O OS fingerprinting -A aggressive (OS+version+scripts+traceroute) --script vuln run vulnerability NSE scripts # Targeting and evasion -p 1-65535 port range -T0…-T5 timing (slow→fast) -f fragment packets -D decoys -oN / -oX / -oG output: normal / XML / grepable

By default Nmap scans the top 1000 most common ports; -p- scans all 65,535. The output flags matter for real work — saving results as XML (-oX) or grepable (-oG) lets findings feed into other tools and reports.

The supporting cast

🖥️ Zenmap

Nmap's official graphical front-end. It exposes Nmap's power through a GUI with saved scan profiles and topology visualization — helpful for learning and for presenting results, though professionals live on the command line.

⚡ Masscan

Built for speed at massive scale — it can scan the entire IPv4 internet for a given port in minutes by sending packets asynchronously. The tool of choice when the target is enormous ranges rather than a few hosts; less detailed than Nmap, so often paired with it.

🔧 hping3

A packet crafter that builds custom TCP/IP packets by hand. Used for fine-grained probing, firewall testing, and understanding exactly how a host responds to specific flag combinations — the tool for when you need surgical control over a single packet.

🔌 Netcat & Angry IP Scanner

Netcat, the "Swiss army knife" of networking, does manual banner grabbing and connection testing. Angry IP Scanner is a fast, friendly cross-platform host-and-port scanner popular for quick network sweeps.

Choosing among them

The selection logic is straightforward. Reach for Nmap for almost everything — it is the default, comprehensive choice. Use masscan when raw speed across huge ranges matters, then follow up with Nmap for detail on what it finds. Use hping3 when you need to craft specific packets or test a firewall's behavior precisely. Use Netcat for quick manual interaction with a service, and Zenmap or Angry IP Scanner when a GUI helps. As with the reconnaissance tools in Module 2, the craft is not memorizing every flag but knowing which tool answers the question in front of you — and for network scanning, that answer is usually Nmap.

🛡️ Countermeasures

💡Think of it like this

You cannot stop people trying the handles; you decide how much the handle tells them.

Close the ports you don't need, which is the cheapest control in security. Filter what remains and make it uninformative — no version banners, consistent responses to malformed input. Rate limiting and an IDS turn a scan from invisible into an alert you can act on.

Where the analogy breaks down: A locked door still confirms a door exists. Blocking scans wholesale also blocks legitimate monitoring, so the aim is raising cost and gaining visibility, not achieving invisibility.

You can't stop scanning — but you can blunt and detect it

Any internet-facing system will be scanned; automated scanning of the entire internet is constant, and a determined attacker within an engagement's scope cannot be prevented from probing. So the defensive goal is not to make scanning impossible but to minimize what it reveals, detect it when it happens, and slow it down. A well-defended target still gets scanned — but the scan returns little of value, trips an alert, and gives defenders time to respond. Unlike footprinting, scanning does touch your systems, which means — unlike footprinting — you can actually see it and act on it.

Reduce the attack surface

The most fundamental countermeasure is to give a scanner as little to find as possible. Every open port is a potential entry point, so close every port and disable every service that isn't strictly needed. A host exposing only the two or three services essential to its role presents a far smaller attack surface than one running a dozen forgotten daemons. This is the principle of least functionality, and it does more to limit scanning's value than any detection system: a port that isn't open can't be exploited, and a service that isn't running can't be fingerprinted. Regular internal scanning of your own systems — running Nmap against yourself, exactly as the attacker would — is how you find and close the exposures you forgot about.

Filter, detect, and respond

🧱 Firewalls

Properly configured firewalls filter and drop unsolicited probes, so ports appear filtered rather than open or closed — denying the scanner clean information. Stateful firewalls that track connection state defeat many of the stealth scans that rely on unusual flags.

🚨 IDS / IPS

Intrusion-detection and -prevention systems recognize scan signatures — bursts of connection attempts, sequential port probing, improbable flag combinations — and alert or block. Because scanning precedes attack, catching a scan is an early warning that someone is taking an interest.

🍯 Honeypots

Decoy systems that exist only to be scanned and attacked. Any interaction with a honeypot is inherently suspicious (no legitimate user has reason to touch it), so it's a high-signal way to detect and study reconnaissance. Covered in depth in a later module.

⏱️ Rate limiting

Throttling connection attempts from a single source slows scanning to a crawl and makes fast scans conspicuous — while also frustrating the attacker's need for speed. It won't stop a patient "slow and low" scan, but it raises the cost of a thorough one.

Tuning detection for evasion

Because attackers use the evasion techniques from earlier in this module, defenders must tune their detection to catch them rather than only the obvious cases. That means configuring IDS/IPS to reassemble fragmented packets before inspection (defeating -f), to flag improbable flag combinations (catching FIN/NULL/Xmas scans), to correlate probes across many source addresses (seeing through decoys), and to watch for slow, distributed patterns over long windows (catching -T0/-T1 scans that stay under simple thresholds). A detection system tuned only for a noisy default scan gives a false sense of security against a skilled adversary — which is precisely why understanding offensive evasion is a defensive necessity.

The bigger picture

Scanning countermeasures come together as layered defense: a minimized attack surface means the scan finds little; firewalls mean much of it is filtered; IDS/IPS and honeypots mean the scan is detected; rate limiting means it is slow; and tuned detection means even an evasive scan is caught. No single control is sufficient, but together they ensure that an attacker's reconnaissance-into-scanning progression is expensive, noisy, and low-yield. And detection matters beyond this phase: because scanning is an early step in the kill chain, catching it is a chance to stop an attack long before exploitation. With the target now scanned — its live hosts, open ports, services, and versions all identified — the attacker's natural next move is to interrogate those services in depth for the specific information they leak, which is the subject of Module 4: Enumeration.
