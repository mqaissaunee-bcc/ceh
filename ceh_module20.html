CEH v13 Module 20: Cryptography

Cryptography

The mathematical foundation beneath every defense in the course: symmetric and asymmetric encryption, hashing and integrity, PKI and digital signatures, the implementation attacks that break real systems, and the post-quantum transition already underway against Harvest-Now-Decrypt-Later.

🔐 Overview

The foundation beneath everything

This is the final module of the course, and there's a reason it comes last: cryptography is the mathematical foundation beneath nearly every defense you've studied. The TLS that protected the web applications in Module 14. The WPA3 handshake from the wireless module. The hashing behind the password storage in system hacking. The signatures that verify software updates, the encryption that protects cloud data at rest, the certificates that establish trust across the internet — all of it is cryptography. Where the other nineteen modules showed you how systems are attacked and defended, this one explains the machinery that makes defense possible in the first place. Understanding it turns a collection of security practices into a coherent whole.

What cryptography provides

At its core, cryptography is the science of protecting information using mathematics, and it delivers four security goals that recur throughout the course:

🔒 Confidentiality

Only authorized parties can read the data. Encryption scrambles information so that anyone intercepting it sees meaningless ciphertext.

✓ Integrity

Tampering can be detected. Hashing ensures that any change to data — even a single bit — is noticeable, so you know if something was altered.

🪪 Authentication

Identities can be verified. Cryptography proves that a message came from who it claims to, and that a server is really who it says it is.

✍️ Non-repudiation

A party can't credibly deny an action. Digital signatures bind an action to an identity so that, for example, a signer can't later disown a signature.

The three building blocks

Nearly all of cryptography is built from three kinds of primitive, and the module is organized around them. Symmetric encryption uses one shared key for both scrambling and unscrambling — fast, and ideal for protecting bulk data. Asymmetric encryption uses a public/private key pair to solve the problem of how two parties who've never met can communicate securely, and it enables digital signatures. Hashing is a one-way transformation used to verify integrity and store passwords safely. Real systems combine all three — HTTPS uses asymmetric cryptography to exchange a symmetric key and hashing to verify integrity, all in one connection. Learn these three primitives and how they combine, and the rest of cryptography becomes readable.

The ethical hacker's angle

Why does an ethical hacker study cryptography? Not usually to break the mathematics — AES and RSA are, for practical purposes, unbreakable head-on. The tester's real work is finding where cryptography is used incorrectly: obsolete algorithms still in service, weak or default keys, poor randomness, unencrypted data that should be protected, and above all mistakes in key management. These implementation failures are common and devastating, and spotting them is a core assessment skill. A recurring theme of this module is that you attack the implementation, not the algorithm — the lock is strong, so you look for the door left unlocked beside it.

What this module covers

We work through the three primitives in turn — symmetric encryption, asymmetric encryption and PKI, and hashing and integrity — building up to how they combine into real systems like TLS. Then attacks on cryptography, focused on the implementation failures that break real systems. Then the module's timely centerpiece: post-quantum cryptography, the once-in-a-generation transition now underway as the industry races to replace algorithms that quantum computers will break. We close with best practices — and, since this is the course's final section, a look back at how cryptography underpins everything that came before it.

🔑 Symmetric Encryption

One key, shared

Symmetric encryption is the oldest and most intuitive form of cryptography: a single secret key is used both to encrypt the data and to decrypt it. If you and I share the same key, I can scramble a message with it and you can unscramble it — and anyone without the key sees only ciphertext, meaningless noise. The concept is thousands of years old (simple ciphers date to antiquity), but modern symmetric algorithms are extraordinarily strong. Its great virtues are speed and efficiency: symmetric encryption is fast enough to protect huge volumes of data in real time, which is why it does the heavy lifting of encrypting the actual content in almost every secure system — files, disks, database fields, and the bulk of network traffic.

AES and the obsolete algorithms

Knowing which algorithms are current and which are dead is directly useful in assessments, because obsolete crypto is a frequent finding.

✅ AES — the standard

The Advanced Encryption Standard, typically with 128- or 256-bit keys, is the modern symmetric workhorse — fast, trusted worldwide, and with no practical break. When you see AES-256, you're looking at strong, current cryptography. It secures everything from HTTPS to disk encryption.

❌ DES & 3DES — obsolete

The Data Encryption Standard's 56-bit key is far too short — it can be brute-forced quickly with modern hardware. Triple DES (3DES) chained three DES operations to extend its life but is now deprecated too. Finding either in use is a security finding.

Other algorithms exist (ChaCha20 is a modern, fast stream cipher used alongside AES, and older ones like RC4 are now broken and banned), but AES is the default answer for symmetric encryption today.

How block ciphers work: modes and IVs

AES is a block cipher: it encrypts data in fixed-size blocks. But real data is longer than one block, and how you apply the cipher across many blocks — the mode of operation — matters enormously for security. The simplest mode, ECB, encrypts each block independently, which leaks patterns: identical plaintext blocks produce identical ciphertext, so structure in the data shows through (the infamous "ECB penguin" image, still recognizable after ECB encryption, illustrates this). Secure modes like CBC and GCM chain or randomize the blocks so patterns vanish, and they use an initialization vector (IV) — a random starting value — to ensure that encrypting the same data twice yields different ciphertext. GCM additionally provides authentication, detecting tampering as well as hiding content. The practical lesson: the algorithm alone isn't enough; the mode and a properly random, never-reused IV are part of what makes encryption secure.

The key distribution problem

Symmetric encryption has one profound limitation, and naming it sets up the entire next section. If security depends on a shared secret key, how do two parties who have never met securely agree on that key? You can't just send the key over the same channel you're trying to protect — an eavesdropper would capture it. For most of history this was solved by physically exchanging keys (couriers, code books), which doesn't scale to a world of billions of strangers transacting online in milliseconds. This key distribution problem was the great unsolved obstacle of cryptography — until asymmetric cryptography provided an elegant answer in the 1970s. That breakthrough is where we turn next.

🔐 Asymmetric Encryption & PKI

Two keys instead of one

Asymmetric cryptography — also called public-key cryptography — was a genuine breakthrough, and it works on a beautifully counterintuitive idea: instead of one shared key, each party has a pair of mathematically linked keys. A public key, which can be shared with anyone, and a private key, kept absolutely secret. The two are related such that data encrypted with one can only be decrypted with the other. This asymmetry — different keys for the two operations — is what gives the technique its power and its name, and it elegantly solves the key distribution problem that stumped symmetric cryptography for millennia.

The two things it enables

The public/private pair enables two distinct and equally important capabilities, and it's worth keeping them separate in your mind:

🔒 Encryption to someone

Anyone can encrypt a message using your public key, and only you — holder of the matching private key — can decrypt it. No prior shared secret needed. This is how a stranger can send you something only you can read.

✍️ Digital signatures

You sign data with your private key, and anyone can verify the signature with your public key. This proves the data came from you and wasn't altered — providing authentication, integrity, and non-repudiation at once.

The main algorithms are RSA (based on the difficulty of factoring large numbers), Diffie-Hellman (a method for securely agreeing on a shared key over an open channel), and ECC (Elliptic Curve Cryptography, which achieves the same security as RSA with much smaller keys, making it efficient for mobile and modern systems). All rely on mathematical problems that are easy to compute in one direction but infeasible to reverse — a property quantum computing threatens, as we'll see.

The hybrid model: how TLS actually works

Here's where the pieces snap together into the system behind HTTPS. Asymmetric cryptography solves key distribution but is slow — too slow to encrypt an entire video stream or web session. Symmetric cryptography is fast but needs a shared key. So real systems use both in a hybrid approach: use asymmetric cryptography once, at the start, to authenticate the parties and securely establish a shared symmetric key; then switch to fast symmetric encryption (AES) for the actual data.

1. Asymmetric
Authenticate & exchange a key
→
2. Symmetric
Encrypt the session data fast

This is exactly what happens every time you connect to an HTTPS website: a brief asymmetric handshake establishes trust and a session key, then AES protects everything after. It's the best of both worlds, and it's the model behind TLS, VPNs, SSH, and secure messaging.

PKI: the trust problem

Asymmetric cryptography has a subtle gap: if you download a public key claiming to belong to your bank, how do you know it's really the bank's and not an attacker's? This is the trust problem, and the answer is Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) — the system of certificates and authorities that binds public keys to verified identities. A digital certificate is essentially a public key plus identity information, signed by a trusted Certificate Authority (CA). Your browser trusts a set of root CAs; when a website presents a certificate signed by one of them, the browser can verify the signature and trust that the public key really belongs to that site. This chain of trust — CA vouches for certificate, certificate binds key to identity — is what makes the padlock in your browser meaningful, and it's the invisible infrastructure underpinning trust across the entire internet.

#️⃣ Hashing & Integrity

The one-way function

The third cryptographic primitive is fundamentally different from the first two: hashing doesn't encrypt anything, because there's no key and nothing to decrypt. A cryptographic hash function takes any input — a word, a file, a whole disk image — and produces a fixed-size output called a digest or hash (SHA-256 always produces 256 bits, whatever the input size). Its defining property is that it is one-way: computing the hash from the input is easy, but recovering the input from the hash is infeasible. It's a cryptographic fingerprint — a small, fixed value that uniquely represents the data but can't be turned back into it.

The properties that make it useful

A good cryptographic hash function has three essential properties, and together they make it invaluable:

➡️ One-way

Given a hash, you can't feasibly find the input that produced it. This is what lets you store a hash without exposing the original data.

💥 Collision-resistant

You can't feasibly find two different inputs that produce the same hash. This ensures a hash uniquely identifies its input.

🌊 Avalanche effect

Changing even a single bit of input completely changes the output. This is what makes hashing detect the tiniest tampering.

SHA-256 (part of the SHA-2 family) and SHA-3 are the current standards. Critically, MD5 and SHA-1 are broken — researchers can produce collisions for them — and must never be used for security purposes, though you'll still find them in legacy systems (another common assessment finding).

What hashing is for

Two uses dominate. First, integrity verification: because any change to data changes its hash, comparing a file's current hash to a known-good value tells you instantly whether it's been altered or corrupted. This is why downloads publish hash values, why forensic evidence is hashed to prove it wasn't tampered with, and how systems detect unauthorized file changes. Second, password storage: systems should never store your actual password. Instead they store its hash; when you log in, they hash what you typed and compare. A database breach then exposes only hashes, not passwords — the one-way property means the attacker can't simply reverse them. This connects directly back to the password-cracking material in the system hacking module, where attackers attack these stored hashes.

Salting: doing password hashing right

Plain hashing of passwords isn't enough, and understanding why teaches an important lesson. If two users have the same password, a plain hash produces the same digest for both — and attackers precompute enormous tables of hashes (rainbow tables) to reverse common passwords instantly. The fix is a salt: a unique random value added to each password before hashing. Salting ensures that identical passwords produce different hashes, and it defeats precomputed rainbow tables entirely, because the attacker would need a separate table for every possible salt. Modern practice goes further, using slow, purpose-built password hashing functions — bcrypt, scrypt, and Argon2 — deliberately designed to be computationally expensive, so that brute-forcing guesses is painfully slow for an attacker even though a single legitimate login is fast enough. The combination — a slow function plus a unique salt — is what correct password storage looks like in 2026.

HMAC: hashing with a key

One more construction ties hashing to authentication. An HMAC (Hash-based Message Authentication Code) combines a hash function with a secret key to prove both that a message is intact and that it came from someone who holds the key. Where a plain hash verifies integrity, an HMAC verifies integrity and authenticity together, which is why it's used throughout secure protocols to protect messages from both tampering and forgery. With all three primitives — symmetric, asymmetric, and hashing — now in hand, we can turn to how cryptographic systems actually get broken.

💥 Attacks on Cryptography

You don't break the math — you break the use of it

The single most important idea in this section is one the whole module has been building toward: strong algorithms are almost never broken mathematically — real systems fail at how cryptography is implemented and used. No one is factoring RSA-2048 or brute-forcing AES-256 by force; those are, for practical purposes, secure. Yet cryptographic failures cause breaches constantly. The resolution to that paradox is that attackers target the edges: the weak key, the obsolete algorithm, the reused value, the leaked timing, the mismanaged secret. For an ethical hacker, this reframes the whole subject — you're not a mathematician attacking a cipher, you're an investigator finding where the cipher was used wrong.

Attacks on the algorithm itself

These are the "textbook" attacks, useful to know even though the strong algorithms resist them:

🔨 Brute force

Trying every possible key. Defeated entirely by adequate key length — brute-forcing AES-256 is infeasible with any conceivable classical computer — but devastating against short keys like DES's 56-bit key.

🧮 Cryptanalysis

Using mathematical weaknesses to break a cipher faster than brute force. This is how algorithms like DES, MD5, and SHA-1 were eventually broken — and why we retire algorithms as analysis advances.

📚 Rainbow tables

Precomputed tables that reverse unsalted password hashes instantly. Defeated by salting, as covered in the hashing section — a direct link between an attack and its countermeasure.

🎂 Birthday attack

Exploits probability to find hash collisions faster than expected, driving the need for sufficiently long hash outputs. The math behind why 256-bit hashes, not 64-bit, are needed.

Implementation attacks — where systems really fail

These are the failures that break real systems, and they're where a tester finds actual vulnerabilities:

🔑 Weak keys & poor randomness

Predictable or default keys, and especially bad random number generation — if the "random" values used to make keys are predictable, the keys can be reproduced. Poor randomness has broken many real systems.

♻️ Reused values

Reusing an IV, nonce, or one-time value that must be unique can catastrophically break otherwise-strong encryption, leaking data or keys. A subtle but recurring real-world failure.

⏱️ Side-channel attacks

Extracting secrets from physical leakage — timing, power consumption, electromagnetic emissions — rather than the algorithm. A cipher can be mathematically perfect yet leak its key through how long it takes to run.

📉 Downgrade & obsolete algorithms

Forcing a connection to fall back to a weak algorithm (as WPA3's downgrade attack did), or simply finding DES, MD5, or SSL still in use. Obsolete crypto in production is a frequent finding.

Key management: the hardest problem

Above all these looms the single biggest practical weakness in cryptography: key management. A cryptographic system is only as secure as its keys, and keys are hard to handle correctly across their whole life — generating them with good randomness, distributing them securely, storing them safely, rotating them periodically, and destroying them when done. The failures are everywhere and often mundane: keys hardcoded in source code (recall the mobile and cloud modules), private keys left in public repositories, keys never rotated, keys shared too widely, keys stored next to the data they protect. An attacker who obtains the key doesn't need to break anything — they simply decrypt. This is why, in practice, cryptographic security is largely a key management problem, and why so much of the defense in the next sections is really about protecting keys.

The man-in-the-middle angle

Finally, a connection back to earlier modules: many "crypto" attacks are really attacks on the trust around cryptography. A man-in-the-middle attacker who can insert themselves into a connection (as in the sniffing, session-hijacking, and wireless modules) may try to present their own key or certificate, or strip encryption entirely, tricking a victim into a connection the attacker can read. This is precisely what PKI and certificate validation defend against — and why failures to validate certificates properly (seen in the mobile module) are so dangerous. Cryptography's strength assumes you're really talking to who you think; defeating that assumption, rather than the math, is often the most practical attack. Next, the one development that genuinely threatens the mathematics itself: quantum computing.

⚛️ Post-Quantum Cryptography

The one thing that threatens the math

Everything so far assumed that certain mathematical problems — factoring huge numbers, computing discrete logarithms — are effectively impossible to solve quickly. That assumption is what makes RSA and ECC secure. Quantum computing threatens to overturn it. This is the most significant development in cryptography in decades, a genuine paradigm shift, and CEH v13 rightly treats post-quantum awareness as essential knowledge for 2026. It's a fitting near-final topic, because it's where the mathematical foundations you've just learned meet their one real challenge — and where the security field is currently mobilizing on a massive scale.

What quantum computers break — and what they don't

The threat is specific, and getting the specificity right is the key insight. A sufficiently powerful quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could efficiently solve the factoring and discrete-logarithm problems that asymmetric cryptography relies on — breaking RSA, ECC, and Diffie-Hellman. But the picture differs sharply by type:

🔴 Asymmetric — broken

RSA, ECC, and Diffie-Hellman would fall to Shor's algorithm. This means key exchange and digital signatures — the trust layer of the internet — need entirely new algorithms. This is the urgent problem.

🟢 Symmetric & hashing — resilient

AES and hash functions are far less affected. Grover's algorithm weakens them somewhat, but simply doubling the key size (using AES-256) restores full security. Your bulk encryption is largely fine.

So the migration is not a wholesale replacement of all cryptography — it's a targeted transition of asymmetric cryptography: key exchange and signatures. That focus makes an overwhelming problem tractable.

Harvest Now, Decrypt Later — why it's urgent today

Here's the part that surprises people: the quantum threat is already a problem, even though no quantum computer can break RSA yet. The reason is an attack strategy called Harvest Now, Decrypt Later (HNDL). Adversaries — particularly nation-states — are recording encrypted traffic today and simply storing it, planning to decrypt it once quantum computers mature. Collecting the ciphertext requires no quantum capability at all; only the eventual decryption does. This means any data that must remain secret for years — diplomatic cables, medical records, state secrets, long-lived credentials — is already at risk right now, because a copy encrypted today may be readable in 2034. Storage is cheap and the harvest window is open, which is why security professionals treat this as a present concern, not a future one. Every day of delay enlarges the corpus of quantum-vulnerable data already sitting in adversary storage.

The NIST standards and the response

The response is post-quantum cryptography (PQC) — new algorithms based on mathematical problems believed hard even for quantum computers (largely lattice-based). After a multi-year global competition, NIST finalized its first three PQC standards in August 2024, and these are now the algorithms the world is migrating to:

🔑 ML-KEM (FIPS 203)

Based on CRYSTALS-Kyber. A key-encapsulation mechanism replacing RSA and ECDH for key exchange. The direct answer to the HNDL threat on confidentiality.

✍️ ML-DSA (FIPS 204)

Based on CRYSTALS-Dilithium. A digital-signature algorithm replacing RSA and ECDSA signatures — securing authentication and PKI in the quantum era.

🛡️ SLH-DSA (FIPS 205)

Based on SPHINCS+. A hash-based signature scheme as a conservative fallback, resting on different security assumptions than the lattice-based schemes for extra resilience.

Hybrid deployment and the transition underway

The migration is already visibly happening. The preferred near-term approach is hybrid: combine a classical algorithm with a post-quantum one so that an attacker must break both to succeed — protecting against a flaw in the new, less battle-tested PQC algorithms while still defending against quantum attacks. The de facto first-generation deployment for TLS combines the classical X25519 with the post-quantum ML-KEM. And this isn't theoretical: by late 2025, over half of human-initiated traffic on Cloudflare's network already used post-quantum key agreement; Google has integrated PQC across Chrome and its services; AWS offers hybrid post-quantum TLS; and Apple's PQ3 protocol added post-quantum security to iMessage. Governments are setting deadlines — NSA's CNSA 2.0 mandates the transition for national security systems by 2030, with compliance milestones beginning as early as 2027. This is the largest cryptographic transition since the move from DES to AES, and it is happening now.

🛡️ Best Practices

Using cryptography correctly

Because cryptographic failures come from misuse rather than broken math, best practices are about using strong primitives correctly. The good news is that the guidance is clear and stable, and it follows directly from everything in this module. A handful of principles prevent the overwhelming majority of real cryptographic vulnerabilities.

The core principles

✅ Use strong, current algorithms

AES-256 for symmetric, RSA-2048+ or ECC for asymmetric, SHA-256/SHA-3 for hashing, bcrypt/scrypt/Argon2 for passwords. Retire DES, 3DES, RC4, MD5, and SHA-1 wherever found.

🚫 Never roll your own crypto

The cardinal rule. Use well-vetted, standard libraries and implementations rather than writing your own — homemade cryptography is almost always broken in subtle ways. Even experts defer to peer-reviewed implementations.

🔑 Manage keys rigorously

Generate keys with strong randomness, never hardcode them, store them securely (ideally in a key vault or HSM), rotate them, and scope them tightly. Key management is where most systems actually fail.

🎲 Get randomness right

Use cryptographically secure random number generators for keys, IVs, and nonces, and never reuse values meant to be unique. Poor randomness silently undermines otherwise-strong cryptography.

Deploy and prepare

Beyond algorithm and key choices, correct deployment matters. Encrypt data both in transit and at rest — use TLS for everything on the wire (the sniffing module showed what happens without it) and encrypt sensitive stored data (the cloud and mobile modules showed the cost of not doing so). Validate certificates properly, since failing to do so defeats the trust PKI provides. And now, prepare for the post-quantum transition: inventory where your systems use asymmetric cryptography, favor crypto-agility (building systems so algorithms can be swapped without rewrites), and begin adopting hybrid PQC for long-lived sensitive data — because Harvest Now, Decrypt Later means the clock is already running. These steps turn the module's knowledge into a defensive posture.

The through-line

Cryptography reduces to a clear message: the mathematics is strong, so security depends on using it correctly — strong current algorithms, standard implementations, rigorous key management, good randomness, and proper deployment, with the post-quantum transition now added to the list. An ethical hacker's role is to find where these break down — the obsolete cipher, the leaked key, the unvalidated certificate — because that, not the algorithm, is where real systems fail. Get the fundamentals right and cryptography delivers on its promise: confidentiality, integrity, authentication, and non-repudiation, the guarantees that every other defense in this course ultimately rests upon.

Completing the course

This is the final module, so it's worth stepping back. Across twenty modules, this course moved from the foundations of ethical hacking and reconnaissance, through scanning, enumeration, and vulnerability analysis, into system hacking, malware, sniffing, social engineering, and denial-of-service; then through session hijacking, evasion, and the full web-application cluster; out to wireless, mobile, IoT and OT; up to the cloud; and finally down to the cryptography beneath it all. And that last move is the point of ending here: cryptography is the foundation the entire edifice stands on. The TLS in the web modules, the WPA3 in wireless, the password hashes in system hacking, the certificate validation in mobile, the encryption of cloud data at rest — every defense you studied is cryptography applied. Having reached the bottom of the stack, you can now see the whole of it as one connected structure.

The ethical hacker's craft, in the end, is to understand all of this well enough to find where it fails — and, in finding those failures under authorization and reporting them responsibly, to help build systems that hold. That is the discipline this course has been teaching throughout: not how to break things, but how to understand them deeply enough to make them secure. With all twenty domains covered, you have the map of that discipline. The work now is practice, depth, and the ethical commitment to use it well.
