CEH v13 Module 17: Hacking Mobile Platforms

Hacking Mobile Platforms

The device in every pocket as attack surface: the Android and iOS security models, rooting and jailbreaking, mobile-specific vectors from smishing to malicious apps, the OWASP Mobile Top 10 (2024), the MobSF/Frida toolkit, MDM in the enterprise, and the hygiene that defends it.

📱 Overview

The most personal target there is

The previous module attacked the airwaves that carry most mobile traffic; this one attacks the devices at the end of those connections. And what devices they are. A modern smartphone concentrates more sensitive data than most laptops ever held — banking apps, authentication tokens, corporate email, health records, private messages, and photos — and wraps it in a package with a camera, a microphone, GPS, and always-on connectivity, carried everywhere and easily lost or stolen. That combination of enormous value, rich sensors, constant connectivity, and physical vulnerability makes mobile devices among the highest-value targets in security, and mobile security one of the fastest-growing areas in the field.

A different security model to attack

Attacking mobile platforms is not just desktop hacking on a smaller screen. Mobile operating systems were designed, from the start, with a fundamentally stronger security posture than traditional desktops: every app runs in a sandbox, isolated from other apps and the system, and access to sensitive resources is gated by a permission model. Code is signed, app stores vet submissions, and the OS enforces boundaries the desktop world never had by default. This changes the nature of the attacks. Rather than the wide-open exploitation of a classic PC, mobile attacks tend to be attempts to escape the sandbox, abuse the permission model, trick the user, or exploit the app rather than the OS. Understanding that model is the prerequisite for everything else, which is why the next section covers it in detail.

Where the weaknesses actually are

Given a hardened OS, where do real mobile compromises come from? Overwhelmingly, from three places. From the apps — third-party software that stores data insecurely, communicates without proper encryption, hardcodes credentials, or pulls in vulnerable third-party code. From the user — tricked by smishing, granting excessive permissions, installing a trojanized app from an unofficial store, or connecting to a malicious network. And from weakened devices — those that have been rooted or jailbroken, stripping away the very protections the platform provides. Notably, the operating systems themselves, while not flawless, are usually the hardest part to break directly; the softer targets are the ecosystem around them. This shapes the whole module.

What this module covers

We begin with the Android and iOS security models — sandboxing, permissions, code signing, and the meaning of rooting and jailbreaking. Then the attack vectors — the mobile-specific ways devices and apps are compromised, from smishing to malicious apps to insecure app behavior. We work through the OWASP Mobile Top 10 (2024) as the organizing framework for app risk, then the specialized tools of mobile testing. A dedicated section covers MDM and the enterprise — how organizations manage the security of devices in a BYOD world — and we close with countermeasures, where simple hygiene defeats the majority of real attacks. Throughout, the tester's job is to assess the security of mobile apps and deployments; the defender's, to keep the most personal device secure.

Test your own devices and apps only. Analyzing, reverse-engineering, or attacking mobile apps and devices you don't own or lack explicit written permission to test is illegal, and mobile devices carry deeply personal data belonging to real people. Practice on your own devices, on emulators, on apps you've built, and on the deliberately-vulnerable mobile apps made for training (such as OWASP's iGoat and the Damn Vulnerable applications). Reverse-engineering commercial apps may also violate their terms of service and local law.

📱 Android & iOS Security Models

Two platforms, one core idea

The mobile world is a duopoly — Android and iOS — and while they differ in philosophy, they share the same foundational security model. Both isolate apps in sandboxes so one app cannot freely read another's data or tamper with the system. Both mediate access to sensitive resources — camera, microphone, location, contacts, storage — through a permission model the user must engage with. Both require apps to be code-signed, so the system can verify an app's origin and integrity. And both distribute apps primarily through a curated store that vets submissions. These four mechanisms — sandbox, permissions, code signing, store review — are the pillars of mobile security, and most attacks are attempts to weaken or circumvent one of them.

Android and iOS: different philosophies

🤖 Android — open

Open-source, running on hardware from many manufacturers, and more flexible: it permits sideloading apps from outside the Play Store and offers users and developers deep access. That openness is powerful but widens the attack surface — sideloaded APKs bypass store vetting, and the fragmented ecosystem means many devices run old, unpatched OS versions. Android's app packages are APK files, which are comparatively straightforward to decompile and analyze.

🍎 iOS — closed

Closed and tightly controlled by Apple, running only on Apple hardware, with app installation locked to the App Store by default and a more restrictive sandbox. This "walled garden" reduces the attack surface and keeps most devices current, at the cost of flexibility. iOS apps (IPA files) and the stricter runtime make analysis harder — often requiring a jailbroken device to inspect deeply.

Neither model is simply "more secure" — iOS's closed model reduces exposure but concentrates trust in Apple, while Android's openness invites more risk but also more scrutiny. A tester must understand both, because organizations run both.

Rooting and jailbreaking: removing the guardrails

The single most important device-level concept in this module is what happens when users deliberately remove the platform's restrictions. Rooting (Android) and jailbreaking (iOS) are the platform-specific names for the same act: gaining privileged, administrative (root) control over the device by defeating the manufacturer's built-in limits. Users do it for legitimate-feeling reasons — customization, installing unapproved apps, removing bloatware — but the security cost is severe: it breaks the sandbox and code-signing guarantees the entire model depends on. A rooted or jailbroken device can install unsigned code, apps can escape their isolation, and the protections that make mobile secure are stripped away.

This is why rooting/jailbreaking is a two-sided topic. For an attacker, a rooted device is a far easier target, and gaining root is often the goal of an exploit. For a security-conscious app or enterprise, detecting a rooted/jailbroken device is essential — which is exactly why banking apps refuse to run on them and MDM systems flag them as non-compliant.

Why the OS is the hard part

It's worth stating plainly, because it directs a tester's effort: directly exploiting a fully-patched, non-rooted iOS or Android OS is hard. These platforms have had two decades of security investment, and full remote OS compromises are rare and valuable enough that working exploits sell for large sums. For most real-world mobile attacks, the path of least resistance runs elsewhere — through a poorly-written app, a tricked user, or a device the user themselves has weakened by rooting. Keeping this in mind stops testers from burning effort against the hardened core when the ecosystem around it offers far softer targets. The next section maps those targets: the mobile attack vectors.

💥 Attack Vectors

The many doors into a phone

Because the OS itself is hard to break directly, mobile attacks come through a wide range of other doors — the device's many connections, its apps, and its user. It helps to group them into four families: network-based, app-based, device-based, and social. A tester assessing a mobile deployment considers all four, because a real attacker will use whichever is weakest.

Network and connectivity attacks

A phone is a bundle of radios — cellular, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, NFC — and each is an attack surface. The evil twin and rogue AP attacks from the wireless module apply directly: connect a phone to a malicious network and its traffic can be intercepted or manipulated. Bluetooth attacks target devices left discoverable or running vulnerable stacks. NFC (used for payments and tap-to-share) can be abused at close range. And because phones roam constantly between networks, they connect to untrusted Wi-Fi far more than laptops do, making man-in-the-middle interception a persistent risk. Much of what you learned in the sniffing, session-hijacking, and wireless modules lands here — the mobile device is just an unusually mobile client.

App-based attacks

The largest category, because apps are where mobile's real weaknesses concentrate. Several recurring problems appear again and again:

🗄️ Insecure data storage

Apps that store sensitive data — tokens, personal info, even passwords — unencrypted on the device, where malware or anyone with device access can read it. A classic and still-common finding.

📡 Insecure communication

Apps that transmit data without proper TLS, or that fail to validate certificates, allowing a man-in-the-middle to read or alter traffic. Cleartext transmission of sensitive data remains surprisingly frequent.

🔑 Hardcoded credentials

API keys, passwords, or secrets baked into the app's code — trivially recovered by decompiling the app. The #1 OWASP mobile risk for 2024.

🎭 Runtime manipulation & tampering

Modifying how an app runs — hooking methods, patching the binary, altering memory — to bypass security checks, unlock features, or steal data. Especially easy on rooted/jailbroken devices.

Underlying many of these is reverse engineering: because the app runs on the attacker's device, they can decompile it, read its logic, extract its secrets, and repackage it — the mobile echo of "never trust the client." An app's own code is visible to whoever holds the device.

Malicious and trojanized apps

A distinct and serious vector is the malicious app itself. Attackers distribute trojanized apps — legitimate-looking software repackaged with hidden malicious code — most often through third-party app stores and sideloaded APKs that bypass official vetting. Once installed and granted permissions, such an app can intercept SMS (including banking one-time codes), log keystrokes, track location, exfiltrate data, or serve as persistent spyware. This is why the source of an app matters so much, and why "install only from official stores" is a foundational defense: the official stores' vetting, while imperfect, blocks the large majority of this.

Social engineering: smishing and beyond

Finally, the user — reachable on a mobile device through channels a desktop never had. Smishing (SMS phishing) sends texts with malicious links or urgent requests, and works disturbingly well because people trust texts more than email and read them faster. Vishing (voice phishing) exploits the phone's primary function. Malicious links arrive through messaging apps, QR codes lead to hostile sites, and fake app-update prompts trick users into installing malware. Every social-engineering principle from that earlier module applies, but the mobile device multiplies the channels and adds urgency. With the vectors mapped, the OWASP Mobile Top 10 organizes the app-side risks into a framework testers use directly.

🔟 OWASP Mobile Top 10 (2024)

The framework for mobile app risk

Just as the OWASP Top 10 organizes web application risk (Module 14), the OWASP Mobile Top 10 organizes mobile app risk — a ranked, consensus list of the most critical security risks in mobile applications, and the standard framework testers and developers use. The current edition is 2024, the first major update since 2016, and the change is significant: the list broadened from device-level flaws to ecosystem-level risks, most visibly by elevating supply-chain security. Using the 2024 list rather than the long-standing 2016 one matters — the reordering reflects how mobile threats actually shifted over eight years.

The OWASP Mobile Top 10 (2024)

M1 · Improper Credential Usage

Hardcoded credentials, and insecure transmission or storage of credentials. Now #1 — over half of breaches involve compromised credentials.

M2 · Inadequate Supply Chain Security

Vulnerable or malicious third-party SDKs and dependencies. New high entry — one flawed SDK can compromise every app that includes it.

M3 · Insecure Authentication/Authorization

Weak authentication of the user or poor enforcement of what they're allowed to do — including bad session management.

M4 · Insufficient Input/Output Validation

Failing to validate data entering or leaving the app — the mobile home of injection and related flaws.

M5 · Insecure Communication

Poor TLS, missing certificate validation, or cleartext transmission of sensitive data — enabling man-in-the-middle interception.

M6 · Inadequate Privacy Controls

Mishandling personal and sensitive information — a newer emphasis reflecting privacy's rising importance.

M7 · Insufficient Binary Protections

Apps that lack defenses against reverse engineering and tampering, exposing code and secrets. Merges the old code-tampering and reverse-engineering risks.

M8 · Security Misconfiguration

Insecure default settings, excessive permissions, exposed components (like unprotected deep links and activities).

M9 · Insecure Data Storage

Sensitive data stored unprotected on the device. Once #2 in 2016, now M9 — still important, but relatively less than the ecosystem risks that rose above it.

M10 · Insufficient Cryptography

Cryptography attempted but done wrong — weak algorithms, poor key handling. A persistent risk across every edition.

Reading the 2024 shift

Two changes tell the story of how mobile risk evolved. First, Improper Credential Usage rose to #1 — reflecting that the most common way into a mobile-connected system is simply through mishandled credentials, whether hardcoded in an app or stored insecurely. Second, and most tellingly, Inadequate Supply Chain Security debuted at #2. This is the mobile version of a lesson the web modules taught: modern apps are assembled from dozens of third-party SDKs, and a single vulnerable or malicious dependency can compromise every app that includes it. A documented example: a single authentication bypass in a partner SDK can grant an attacker the identity of every partner in that ecosystem, across millions of apps. Meanwhile, older device-level concerns like insecure data storage and weak cryptography slipped down — not because they vanished, but because the ecosystem-level risks grew more urgent.

How testers use it

The Mobile Top 10 is a checklist that structures a mobile app assessment. A tester reverse-engineers the app and inspects it against each category: Are credentials hardcoded (M1)? What third-party SDKs does it pull in, and are they current (M2)? How does it authenticate and manage sessions (M3)? Does it validate input (M4) and use proper TLS (M5)? Does it protect privacy (M6), resist tampering (M7), avoid misconfiguration and exposed components (M8), store data securely (M9), and use cryptography correctly (M10)? Walking the list ensures nothing is missed and gives findings a shared vocabulary that developers recognize. The next section covers the tools that make this inspection possible.

🛠️ Tools

The mobile testing toolkit

Mobile app assessment has its own specialized toolset, organized around a simple idea: because the app runs on a device you control, you can take it apart (static analysis), watch it run (dynamic analysis), and change its behavior (instrumentation). The tools below map onto those three activities, and together they let a tester work through the OWASP Mobile Top 10 against a real app.

The core tools

🔬 MobSF

The Mobile Security Framework — the all-in-one starting point. It performs automated static analysis of APK and IPA files (surfacing hardcoded secrets, insecure settings, dangerous permissions) and dynamic analysis of the running app. Usually the first tool pointed at an app.

🪝 Frida

The dynamic instrumentation toolkit. Frida hooks into a running app to inspect and modify its behavior at runtime — intercepting function calls, bypassing checks (like root detection or certificate pinning), and dumping data. The workhorse of runtime manipulation.

🔧 apktool

Decompiles Android APKs into readable resources and smali code, and rebuilds them — the foundation of reverse-engineering and repackaging Android apps to understand or modify their logic.

🧰 Objection / Drozer

Objection builds on Frida for runtime mobile exploration without needing to write hooks by hand; Drozer probes Android apps for exposed components and attack surface. Both streamline common assessment tasks.

Supporting tools and setup

Several tools from earlier modules carry over. Burp Suite (Module 14) is essential for intercepting an app's network traffic — the standard way to inspect what data an app sends and to test its communication security, often combined with Frida to defeat certificate pinning. Emulators and virtual devices (the Android Emulator, iOS Simulator) provide safe, disposable environments for analysis. For deep iOS work, a jailbroken test device is frequently required, since the closed runtime otherwise blocks inspection. And ADB (Android Debug Bridge) is the fundamental command-line tool for interacting with Android devices — installing apps, pulling files, and running shell commands. Setting up this environment — emulator, proxy, instrumentation — is itself a skill, and the first practical hurdle in mobile testing.

The AI dimension

Reflecting CEH v13's emphasis, AI is increasingly woven into mobile testing. Large language models assist in reverse-engineering and understanding decompiled app code, triaging the findings of automated scanners like MobSF, and reasoning about complex vulnerabilities in unfamiliar codebases. Research is actively exploring LLMs as aids to mobile app privacy and security analysis. As everywhere in the course, these tools accelerate the work on top of the fundamentals — a tester who understands sandboxing, the permission model, and the OWASP categories can direct the AI and verify its output, while the tools handle the tedious decompilation and pattern-matching. Understanding still leads; automation follows.

Analysis requires ownership. Decompiling, instrumenting, or repackaging apps you don't own may violate their terms of service, copyright, and anti-circumvention laws — even when done for learning. Work on your own apps, deliberately-vulnerable training apps, and apps you have written permission to test. Jailbreaking or rooting a device to test it should be done on dedicated test hardware, not a daily driver holding real personal data.

🏢 MDM & the Enterprise

The organizational problem

Everything so far concerned individual devices and apps. Organizations face a harder problem: hundreds or thousands of mobile devices — many of them personally owned — accessing corporate email, documents, and systems. Each is a potential entry point, each is easily lost, and the organization has limited control over what the user installs or which networks they join. This is the BYOD (Bring Your Own Device) reality, and it created the need for a way to enforce security policy across a fleet of devices the company may not even own. That solution is Mobile Device Management.

What MDM does

Mobile Device Management (MDM) is software for centrally administering and securing an organization's mobile devices, distributing configuration and policy over the air. Its core capabilities map directly onto the risks this module has covered:

🔒 Policy enforcement

Mandate device encryption, strong passcodes, and screen locks; enforce OS-update compliance. Ensures the platform's own protections are actually switched on.

🧹 Remote wipe & lock

Remotely lock or erase a lost or stolen device — often just the corporate data (selective wipe) on a personal device — neutralizing the physical-loss risk that makes mobile so exposed.

📦 App control

Control which apps may be installed, push required apps, and blocklist risky ones — reducing the trojanized-app and malicious-app threat.

🚩 Compliance detection

Detect rooted/jailbroken or otherwise non-compliant devices and automatically restrict their access to corporate resources — directly countering the weakened-device risk.

The broader family includes MAM (Mobile Application Management, focused on managing and securing specific apps and their data rather than the whole device) and MTD (Mobile Threat Defense, which actively detects mobile threats). Common MDM/enterprise platforms include Microsoft Intune, VMware Workspace ONE, and Jamf (for Apple fleets).

The BYOD balance

MDM sits on a genuine tension worth understanding: the organization needs to secure its data, but the device belongs to the employee and holds their personal life. Wipe too aggressively and you erase someone's family photos; manage too lightly and corporate data leaks. Modern approaches resolve this by separating work and personal data — containerizing corporate apps and data so the organization controls only its own container, can selectively wipe just that, and leaves personal data untouched. This separation is what makes BYOD workable, and it reflects a mature security mindset: enforce a boundary you can actually control, rather than demanding total control you can't fairly have.

MDM from both sides

For an ethical hacker, MDM is worth understanding from two angles. As a defense, it's the single most effective enterprise control for the mobile risks in this module — its absence in an organization with mobile access to sensitive data is itself a significant finding. As a target, MDM is a high-value system: it has privileged control over every enrolled device, so a compromise of the MDM server could push malicious configuration or apps to the entire fleet. A thorough mobile assessment therefore looks not just at individual devices and apps but at how the organization manages them — and whether that management system is itself secured. With the enterprise picture complete, the final section distills the defenses.

🛡️ Countermeasures

Three audiences, three sets of defenses

Mobile security is defended by three different groups, and it's clearest to organize the countermeasures by who acts: the user holding the device, the developer writing the app, and the organization managing the fleet. Each has a distinct set of responsibilities, and mobile is secure only when all three do their part. The encouraging news, established at the outset, is that the OS itself does much of the heavy lifting — so these defenses are largely about not undermining it and about securing the ecosystem around it.

For the user: hygiene defeats most attacks

The single most important truth in mobile defense is that basic security hygiene stops the large majority of real-world attacks. None of it is exotic:

🔄 Keep everything updated

Patch the OS and apps promptly — this closes the known vulnerabilities that most attacks rely on. Fragmentation makes this harder on Android, so buy devices with good update support.

🏪 Official stores only

Install only from the official App Store or Play Store, avoiding sideloaded APKs and third-party stores — the primary distribution channel for trojanized apps.

🔍 Scrutinize permissions

Question why an app wants a permission — a flashlight app has no need for contacts or location. Grant the minimum, and revoke what isn't needed.

🚫 Don't root/jailbreak

Leave the platform's protections intact. Use strong device authentication (biometrics/long passcode), enable encryption, and be wary of smishing links and unexpected Wi-Fi.

For the developer: build the app securely

The developer's defenses map directly onto the OWASP Mobile Top 10, which is exactly why that framework exists. In practice: never hardcode credentials or secrets in the app (M1); vet and keep current every third-party SDK (M2); implement strong authentication and session management (M3); validate all input and output (M4); use proper TLS with certificate validation for all communication (M5); handle personal data responsibly (M6); add binary protections against tampering and reverse engineering, and detect rooted/jailbroken devices (M7); avoid misconfiguration and exposed components (M8); store sensitive data encrypted, or better, not on the device at all (M9); and use cryptography correctly with strong, standard algorithms (M10). The overarching principle is the mobile form of "never trust the client": since the app runs on hardware the attacker may control, security-critical decisions belong on the server, and anything shipped in the app can be reverse-engineered.

For the organization: manage the fleet

Enterprises add the layer the individual can't: centralized control. MDM/MAM enforces encryption and passcode policy, enables remote wipe, controls apps, and detects non-compliant devices. A clear BYOD policy — ideally with work/personal data separation — defines the boundary between corporate and personal. Mobile application vetting (as NIST recommends) checks apps before they're allowed on managed devices. And user security awareness training extends the social-engineering defenses to the mobile channels — smishing, malicious QR codes, fake update prompts — that target employees directly. Together these give the organization an enforceable security posture across devices it doesn't fully own.

The through-line

Mobile security comes down to a reassuring core idea: the platforms are, by design, quite secure — so the job is mostly to not weaken them and to secure the ecosystem around them. Users keep the guardrails up and practice basic hygiene; developers build apps that don't leak the data the sandbox is trying to protect; organizations enforce a manageable boundary over the fleet. The attacker, unable to break the hardened OS directly, goes for the credential hardcoded in an app, the user tricked by a text, or the device someone rooted for convenience — and every one of those has a straightforward defense. An ethical hacker who understands the platform models, the attack vectors, the OWASP framework, and the enterprise controls can assess a mobile deployment end to end and help secure the most personal computer most people will ever own.
