Overview — what this lab is really teaching

On a switched network you shouldn't be able to see other people's traffic — but ARP has no authentication. Send forged ARP replies and you can convince a victim that you are the gateway, routing their traffic through your machine. That's a man-in-the-middle (MiTM), and it's the active half of Module 8: Sniffing.

Once in the middle you can do more than watch: this lab captures cleartext logins, swaps images, and even rewrites JavaScript in flight. The real takeaway is why that's possible at all — unencrypted traffic has no integrity — and why HTTPS is the control that makes the whole attack fall apart.

The environment you're working in

From Kali you run ettercap against a victim host and the gateway (pfSense) on the LAN. Poisoning both — victim and gateway — is what puts you squarely in the path of the victim's internet traffic. Everything stays inside the pod.

Walkthrough

Task 1 — Get on the LAN, then get in the middle

1 Put Kali on the LAN segment

First the setup step the lab requires: from the network icon, disconnect eth0 and connect eth1. That moves Kali onto the LAN as 192.168.0.2, on the same segment as the victim and the pfSense gateway — you can't be in the middle of a conversation you're not on.

2 Launch ettercap and poison

root@kali:~# ettercap -G

-G opens the graphical interface. Scan for hosts, then add the victim to Target 1 and the gateway to Target 2 and start ARP poisoning. Ettercap now sends each one forged ARP replies, so the victim sends its gateway-bound traffic to you and the gateway sends the replies back through you.

Why you must poison both ends

Poison only the victim and you see traffic going out but not coming back. Poisoning victim and gateway puts you in the full two-way path — a true MiTM.

Task 2 — Harvest and tamper

3 Capture cleartext credentials

By default ettercap watches for HTTP logins and records the usernames and passwords, at the packet level on the wire. The lab tests this by logging into the pfSense web interface from the victim — browse to http://192.168.0.254 and sign in with admin / pfsense — and the credentials appear at the bottom of the ettercap window on Kali. Nothing is decrypted; it's readable because it was never encrypted. (This works for HTTP only; capturing HTTPS needs extra setup and is out of scope.)

4 Rewrite images with a filter

root@kali:~# service apache2 start

Because you're relaying the traffic, you can also change it with an ettercap filter. First start Apache on Kali so it can host the replacement image (logo.jpg in /var/www/html). The lab's logo.filter rewrites every <img src> in HTTP responses to point at http://192.168.0.2/logo.jpg. Load the compiled version via ettercap's Filters → Load a filter → logo.ef (the .filter is the readable source; the .ef is the compiled form ettercap runs). Browse to http://192.168.68.12 from the victim and every image on the page is swapped.

📸 Screenshot for your report

Capture the victim's browser showing all the OWASP page images replaced.

5 Inject JavaScript with another filter

Load a second filter, hacked-alert.ef — its source finds the closing </head> tag and inserts a JavaScript alert(). Browse the OWASP page again and a pop-up fires (and the images are still swapped — filters chain, so you keep each one small and combine them). The alert is harmless here, but the same injection point is how a real attacker would drop a BeEF hook (Lab 11) into the page.

Notice what you can't touch

HTTPS pages don't reveal credentials and can't be silently rewritten — the browser detects the tampering. That failure is the single most important observation in the lab.

The defender's view

Two layers: stop the poisoning, and make the traffic worthless

Switch-level controls stop the MiTM position; encryption makes a MiTM pointless even if achieved.

  • Dynamic ARP Inspection (DAI) with DHCP snooping — the switch drops forged ARP replies that don't match a trusted binding. The direct counter to this attack.
  • Encrypt everything — HTTPS with HSTS, plus SSH and TLS, so intercepted traffic is unreadable and un-modifiable.
  • Port security and network segmentation — limit who can even be on the victim's segment.
  • Static ARP entries for critical hosts where practical.
  • Detect it — arpwatch and IDS alerts on duplicate/ changing MAC-to-IP mappings.

CEH v13 exam relevance

The active-sniffing core of Module 8: Sniffing. High-yield facts:

  • ARP spoofing/poisoning — why it works (ARP is stateless and unauthenticated).
  • Active vs. passive sniffing, and why switched networks need active techniques.
  • MiTM capabilities: capture, injection, SSL stripping, DNS spoofing.
  • Defenses: DAI, DHCP snooping, port security, static ARP, encryption.
  • Related layer-2 attacks: MAC flooding, DHCP starvation/spoofing.

Check yourself

1. Why does ARP spoofing work at all?

ARP has no authentication — a host accepts ARP replies without verifying them, so forged replies poison its cache and redirect traffic.

2. Why must you poison both the victim and the gateway?

To see traffic in both directions you must sit in the full path — the victim must send to you, and the gateway must send replies back through you.

3. You're in the middle but can't read a victim's banking login. Why?

It's HTTPS — the traffic is encrypted end-to-end, so a MiTM sees only ciphertext and can't silently modify it.

4. Which switch feature is the direct counter to ARP poisoning?

Dynamic ARP Inspection (DAI), backed by DHCP snooping — it drops ARP replies that don't match a trusted IP-to-MAC binding.

Cheat sheet

The attack

  • ettercap -G — GUI
  • Target 1 = victim · Target 2 = gateway
  • Start ARP poisoning (both ends)
  • Harvest cleartext creds · load content filters
  • Modern: bettercap

The defense

  • DAI + DHCP snooping
  • HTTPS/HSTS, SSH, TLS everywhere
  • Port security, segmentation
  • Static ARP for critical hosts
  • Detect: arpwatch, duplicate-MAC alerts
