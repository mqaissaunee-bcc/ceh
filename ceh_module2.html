Footprinting and Reconnaissance

🔍 Overview

Phase one: know the target before you touch it

Every attack — and every authorized penetration test — begins here. Footprinting (also called reconnaissance) is the process of systematically gathering information about a target organization, its networks, its systems, and its people before any active engagement begins. It is the first phase of the ethical hacking methodology, and it maps directly to the Reconnaissance stage of the Cyber Kill Chain from Module 1. The goal is to build as complete a picture of the target as possible using information that is, in most cases, freely and legally available — turning scattered public data into a coherent map that guides everything that follows.

It is tempting for newcomers to rush past reconnaissance to get to the "real" hacking, but experienced testers know the opposite is true: the field's rule of thumb is that the large majority of successful attacks begin with thorough reconnaissance, and poor recon leads to failed attacks. Time invested in mapping the target thoroughly makes every later phase — scanning, enumeration, exploitation — faster, more accurate, and more likely to succeed. Reconnaissance is often the most time-consuming phase precisely because it is the most valuable.

Why information is the attacker's first weapon

Consider what an attacker learns from public sources alone: the domains and IP ranges an organization owns, the technologies it runs (from job postings and website headers), the names and email formats of its employees (from social media and the company site), its physical locations, its business relationships, and often sensitive files it never meant to expose. Each fact is a thread. Employee names feed social engineering. The technology stack points to specific exploits. Exposed subdomains reveal forgotten servers. An email naming convention lets an attacker guess valid addresses for a phishing campaign. None of this requires touching the target's systems, and much of it is invisible to the target — which is exactly what makes it dangerous.

What footprinting reveals

Reconnaissance targets several categories of information, and a good tester works through all of them:

🌐 Network information

Domain names and subdomains, IP address ranges and netblocks, DNS records, name servers, and the network infrastructure an organization operates. The skeleton of the target's internet presence.

🖥️ System information

Operating systems, web server software and versions, technologies in use, and services exposed to the internet. The clues that later point to specific vulnerabilities.

🏢 Organizational information

Employee names and roles, email addresses and formats, phone numbers, physical locations, org structure, and business relationships. The raw material for social engineering.

📄 Exposed data

Documents with revealing metadata, files left in open directories, credentials in code repositories, and information leaked through misconfiguration. The unintended gifts.

What this module covers

We begin with the methodology — the crucial distinction between passive and active reconnaissance and the disciplined process that structures the phase. Then we survey the techniques: search and OSINT (search-engine intelligence, Google dorking, social media, and open-source intelligence gathering) and DNS and network footprinting (WHOIS, DNS interrogation, and mapping infrastructure). We then examine the tools that automate and accelerate the work — theHarvester, Maltego, Shodan, recon-ng, and the modern AI-assisted additions — before closing with the countermeasures an organization uses to shrink and manage its public footprint. Throughout, the emphasis is on building the map that makes the scanning phase (Module 3) and everything after it effective.

Passive gathering is generally legal; active reconnaissance is not, without authorization. Reading public records, search results, and social media touches no one's systems and is broadly permissible. But active footprinting — querying the target's own DNS servers, banner grabbing, or any direct interaction — reaches into systems you do not own and requires explicit written authorization. Active reconnaissance without permission can violate computer-crime laws such as the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act. Know which side of the line each technique falls on.

🧭 Methodology

The defining distinction: passive vs. active

The single most important concept in this module — and one the CEH exam tests heavily — is the distinction between passive and active reconnaissance. It determines both the risk of detection and the legal footing of everything you do, so a professional keeps it in mind constantly.

🕶️ Passive reconnaissance

Gathering information without directly interacting with the target's systems. You read search results, public records, social media, WHOIS databases, and cached pages — all from third parties. The target has no way to detect it, because you never touch their infrastructure. This carries essentially no risk of detection and, in most jurisdictions, no legal risk.

📡 Active reconnaissance

Gathering information by directly interacting with the target's systems — querying their DNS servers, grabbing service banners, probing their web application. This yields richer, more current information, but it leaves traces in the target's logs and can trigger their defenses. It requires authorization, and it begins to blur into the scanning of Module 3.

The practical rule most testers follow: start passive, stay passive as long as possible, and go active only within an authorized scope. Passive recon builds the map invisibly; active recon fills in the details but announces your presence. A skilled tester extracts remarkable amounts of information before ever sending a single packet to the target.

The reconnaissance process

Effective footprinting is systematic, not scattershot. A disciplined process ensures nothing is missed and each finding feeds the next.

Build the picture

It begins with scoping — knowing exactly which domains, ranges, and assets are authorized targets, so you don't stray into systems you have no permission to examine (a partner's network, a cloud provider's shared infrastructure). Then comes passive collection, casting a wide net across public sources. Only within an authorized scope does active collection follow, directly querying the target to confirm and enrich what passive recon suggested. Finally, analysis turns the raw findings into a coherent map — correlating an IP range with a set of subdomains, matching employee names to an email format, connecting a job posting's tech stack to a specific server. That map is the deliverable that drives the scanning phase.

OSINT: the engine of passive recon

Most passive reconnaissance is a form of OSINT — Open-Source Intelligence — the collection and analysis of information from publicly available sources. The term comes from the intelligence community, and the discipline is the same: individually, public facts seem harmless; correlated and analyzed, they reveal far more than any single source intended. A LinkedIn profile names an employee and their role. The company website reveals the email format. A conference talk names the security tools in use. A code repository leaks an internal hostname. Separately, trivia; together, a detailed operational picture. The art of OSINT is not just finding information but connecting it, and it is why relationship-mapping tools like Maltego (covered in the tools section) are so valued. This is also the sobering lesson for defenders: an organization's public footprint is far larger, and far more revealing, than it usually realizes.

🔍 Search & OSINT Techniques

The search engine as a reconnaissance tool

The most accessible reconnaissance tool in the world is the ordinary search engine — but used with skill, it becomes something far more powerful than a way to find websites. Search engines have indexed vast swaths of the internet, including a great deal that organizations never intended to be public: exposed documents, open directories, login portals, configuration files, and error messages leaking internal detail. The technique of using advanced search operators to surface this material is called Google dorking (or Google hacking), and it is one of the most productive passive-recon methods there is.

Google dorking: operators that unlock hidden data

Advanced search operators narrow results with precision, letting a tester pinpoint exactly the kind of exposed information that matters. A handful of operators do most of the work:

site:example.com # restrict results to a single domain site:example.com filetype:pdf # find PDF files on that domain site:example.com inurl:admin # find URLs containing "admin" (login pages) intitle:"index of" # find open directory listings site:example.com ext:sql # find exposed database dumps cache:example.com # view a cached copy of a page

The real power comes from combining operators: site:example.com filetype:xlsx hunts for exposed spreadsheets on one domain; intitle:"index of" "backup" finds open directories containing backups. The community-maintained Google Hacking Database (GHDB), hosted by Exploit-DB, catalogs thousands of ready-made dorks proven to surface exposed cameras, credentials, configuration files, and vulnerable systems. For a defender, the uncomfortable takeaway is that anything a web server exposes is likely indexed and findable — which is why the countermeasures section stresses controlling what is published in the first place.

Social media and people intelligence

Social media has become one of the richest reconnaissance sources, because people and organizations publish an extraordinary amount about themselves. LinkedIn in particular is a goldmine: it reveals employee names, job titles, org structure, tenure, and — through the technologies people list as skills — the target's tech stack. An attacker building a social-engineering campaign can assemble an org chart, identify who works in finance or IT, and infer the email address format, all from public profiles. Other platforms leak location data, personal details usable for password guessing or answers to security questions, and photos that inadvertently reveal badges, screens, or facilities. The lesson runs both ways: this is how attackers profile targets, and it is why security awareness (Module 9) urges caution about what employees share publicly.

Website footprinting and job postings

The target's own website is a primary source. Beyond its visible content, the underlying HTML, HTTP response headers, and linked resources reveal the web server software, frameworks, analytics tools, and third-party services in use. Tools like Netcraft and browser extensions such as Wappalyzer fingerprint a site's technology stack automatically. Archived versions via the Wayback Machine can expose pages and information the organization has since removed — but which never truly disappeared.

Job postings deserve special mention as a deceptively powerful source. To attract qualified candidates, companies list the exact technologies a role requires — naming specific firewalls, databases, cloud platforms, ticketing systems, and often their versions. To an attacker, a job ad is a annotated map of the tech stack and a shopping list of potential vulnerabilities, published voluntarily. The names of hiring managers and team leads in those posts also feed later social engineering. Few organizations think of recruiting as an information-disclosure risk, but it is one of the clearest.

Document metadata and exposed files

Documents an organization publishes — PDFs, Word files, spreadsheets, images — often carry hidden metadata that reveals far more than the visible content: author names and usernames, internal file paths, software versions, and sometimes internal server names. A tool like FOCA automates the extraction of this metadata across all the documents on a domain, quietly assembling a list of usernames and internal systems from files the organization shared freely. Combined with Google dorking to find the documents and metadata extraction to mine them, an attacker can build a surprising inventory of internal detail without ever touching the target's network — a vivid demonstration of how much passive recon alone can yield.

🌐 DNS & Network Footprinting

Mapping the target's infrastructure

Where the previous section gathered organizational and human intelligence, this one maps the technical skeleton: the domains, addresses, and name servers that make up the target's internet presence. This is where footprinting shades from purely passive toward active — some techniques (reading public WHOIS and DNS databases) touch no target system, while others (querying the target's own DNS servers directly) reach into its infrastructure and require authorization. The output is the network map that the scanning phase in Module 3 will probe.

WHOIS: who owns what

WHOIS is a query protocol for the databases that record domain and IP registration. A WHOIS lookup on a domain returns the registrar, the registration and expiry dates, the name servers, and — historically — the registrant's name, email, phone, and address. That contact data was once a rich recon source, though privacy-protection services now redact much of it for many domains. WHOIS on an IP address reveals which organization owns a given block, helping a tester confirm which address ranges belong to the target.

# Look up domain registration details whois example.com # Look up who owns an IP address / netblock whois 203.0.113.10

Related to WHOIS, public BGP routing data exposes the IP ranges (autonomous systems) an organization announces to the internet, letting a tester map the full set of address blocks the target owns — the raw target list for scanning.

DNS interrogation

The Domain Name System is a treasure map of an organization's infrastructure, because its records point to the servers that run the target's services. Querying DNS reveals which hosts exist and what they do:

# Query all common records for a domain dig example.com ANY +noall +answer # Specific record types dig example.com MX # mail servers dig example.com NS # name servers dig example.com TXT # SPF/DKIM and other text records nslookup -type=A mail.example.com # resolve a hostname to an IP

Each record type tells a story. A/AAAA records map hostnames to IP addresses. MX records name the mail servers — targets for email-based attacks. NS records identify the name servers. TXT records often contain SPF and DKIM data that reveals which third-party services (marketing platforms, cloud providers) are authorized to send mail for the domain, exposing business relationships. Discovering subdomains is especially valuable: forgotten dev, staging, or admin subdomains frequently run older, less-protected software and are a classic way in. Testers enumerate subdomains through DNS brute-forcing, certificate-transparency logs, and passive DNS databases.

DNS zone transfers: a classic misconfiguration

A DNS zone transfer is a mechanism by which one name server copies an entire zone's records to another — legitimately used to synchronize a primary server with its secondaries. Misconfigured, it becomes an information leak: if a name server permits a zone transfer to anyone, a tester can request the whole zone and receive a complete inventory of the domain's DNS records, exposing internal hostnames and infrastructure the organization assumed were hidden.

# Attempt a zone transfer against a name server dig axfr example.com @ns1.example.com

Properly configured servers restrict transfers to authorized secondaries, so a successful transfer against an arbitrary requester is a notable finding — and while it rarely works against well-run infrastructure today, it remains a tested concept precisely because the payoff is so large when a misconfiguration exists. Note that requesting a zone transfer directly queries the target's server: this is active reconnaissance, requiring authorization.

Network path and exposure mapping

Two more techniques round out network footprinting. Traceroute maps the network path packets take to reach the target, revealing intermediate routers and often the target's ISP and network topology — useful for understanding how the target connects to the internet and where its perimeter sits. And the search engine Shodan (covered in the tools section) sits at the boundary of footprinting and scanning: it has already indexed the internet's exposed services, so a passive Shodan query on the target's IP ranges reveals open ports, running services, and banners without the tester sending a single packet to the target — a preview of the scanning phase, assembled from someone else's scans. Together, these techniques hand the scanning phase in Module 3 a precise list of addresses, hostnames, and likely services to probe.

Practice these queries safely

Reading whois and dig output is a skill that only comes from running the commands, but running them against a real organization you have no authorization to test is exactly what this module warns against. 🛠️ Tools

Automating and accelerating reconnaissance

Everything in the previous sections can be done by hand, but the volume of information involved makes automation essential. Reconnaissance tools do three things: they collect information from many sources far faster than manual searching, they correlate disparate findings into a coherent picture, and they discover connections a human would miss. Knowing not just what each tool does but when and why to choose one over another is exactly what the CEH exam — and real engagements — demand. Here are the essential tools of the trade.

The core OSINT toolkit

🌾 theHarvester

Collects email addresses, subdomains, and host names from search engines and public data sources in seconds. The fast first step for building a list of an organization's people and infrastructure. Free and scriptable.

🕸️ Maltego

The premier relationship-mapping tool. Uses "transforms" to query public data sources and graph the links between domains, IPs, people, email addresses, and infrastructure. Turns scattered OSINT into a visual picture — ideal for spotting connections and for presenting findings to clients. A Community Edition is free.

🔦 Shodan

The "search engine for devices." Indexes internet-connected systems worldwide, revealing exposed services, banners, default credentials, webcams, and industrial control systems. A passive query against the target's IP ranges surfaces its internet-facing exposure without touching it. Censys serves a similar role.

🧩 recon-ng

A modular reconnaissance framework built like Metasploit — a console with independent modules and a database backing that make large OSINT workflows repeatable and organized. Preferred when reconnaissance needs to be systematic and reportable rather than ad hoc.

Choosing the right tool

Because the exam tests tool selection directly, it helps to hold a mental map of each tool's primary purpose. theHarvester for quickly gathering emails and subdomains. Maltego for mapping relationships between entities. Shodan (and Censys) for discovering internet-exposed services. recon-ng for a structured, modular OSINT workflow. FOCA for extracting metadata from an organization's published documents. Netcraft and Wappalyzer for fingerprinting a website's technology. And the command-line staples — whois, dig, and nslookup — for direct WHOIS and DNS interrogation. A capable tester reaches for whichever fits the question at hand, and often chains several: theHarvester to find subdomains, Shodan to see what's exposed on them, and Maltego to tie it all together.

The AI dimension

CEH v13 explicitly integrates AI into reconnaissance, reflecting how the discipline is changing. AI assistants and integrations (such as ShellGPT for automating command-line recon) now help testers generate search queries, write collection scripts, and — most significantly — synthesize the flood of collected data into a coherent profile far faster than manual analysis. This cuts both ways, the recurring theme of the course: AI accelerates the attacker's reconnaissance and lowers the skill barrier, while defenders use the same capability to monitor their own exposure. The core techniques in this module remain the foundation; AI is an accelerant layered on top, making thorough reconnaissance faster and more accessible than ever.

A note on practice. These techniques should be practiced only against systems you own or are explicitly authorized to test. Platforms like TryHackMe and Hack The Box provide legal, purpose-built environments where reconnaissance is explicitly sanctioned — the right place to build these skills.

🛡️ Countermeasures

Defending against information gathering

Footprinting is uniquely difficult to defend against, because it exploits information the organization has chosen to make public, gathered from third-party sources the organization doesn't control. You cannot block passive reconnaissance the way you block a network attack — there is nothing to block, since the attacker never touches your systems. Instead, defense means minimizing and managing your public footprint: reducing what is exposed, controlling what must be published, and monitoring what is discoverable. The goal is not to become invisible (impossible for any organization that does business online) but to ensure nothing sensitive is needlessly exposed.

Know your own footprint first

The foundational defensive practice is to perform reconnaissance on yourself, regularly and thoroughly. Run the same OSINT tools and Google dorks against your own organization that an attacker would, and see what surfaces: exposed documents, forgotten subdomains, leaked credentials, revealing job postings, oversharing employees. You cannot protect against exposure you don't know about, so this self-assessment — sometimes formalized as an "attack surface management" program — is the starting point for everything else. Search Shodan for your own IP ranges, check the Google Hacking Database against your domains, and review what your published documents reveal in their metadata.

Concrete controls

📄 Control published information

Sanitize document metadata before publishing. Review job postings so they don't over-disclose the tech stack. Avoid putting sensitive internal detail on public pages. Train employees on what is safe to share on social media and professional networks.

🌐 Harden DNS and registration

Restrict DNS zone transfers to authorized secondaries only. Use WHOIS privacy protection to redact registrant contact details. Ensure internal hostnames aren't exposed in public DNS. Audit subdomains and decommission forgotten ones.

🔦 Close exposed services

Regularly check what Shodan and Censys reveal about your internet-facing systems, and close or firewall anything that shouldn't be exposed. Remove default credentials and banners that leak version information.

🗑️ Manage the trail

Remove sensitive files from public servers and open directories. Purge old content from caches and archives where possible. Establish a policy for what may be published and who approves it.

The enduring truth about public information

The most important principle to internalize — for both attacker and defender — is that once information reaches the internet, it may never fully disappear. Caches, archives, screenshots, and re-postings mean that "deleting" exposed data often fails to actually remove it. This is why the emphasis falls so heavily on prevention: it is far more effective to avoid exposing sensitive information in the first place than to try to retract it afterward. A mature program treats every piece of public information as permanent and asks, before publishing, whether it should be public at all.

Reconnaissance defense, then, is really a discipline of awareness and restraint. An organization that knows its own footprint, publishes deliberately, hardens its DNS and exposed services, and trains its people to share carefully denies attackers the easy intelligence that makes every later phase of an attack simpler. It cannot stop footprinting entirely — but it can ensure that what an attacker finds is far less useful. With the target mapped, the natural next step for an attacker is to probe that map directly, which is the subject of Module 3: Scanning Networks.
